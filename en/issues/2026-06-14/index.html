Chapter 01

First, Separate Action from Authority

Story 01

Correct Steps Add Up to the Wrong Result

An agent does not acquire dangerous power in one great leap. It accumulates it one step at a time: opened a file, changed a field, handed the task onward. Individual actions may be sensible and authorized, while the chain as a whole is no longer the assignment the person gave.

Workflow-GYM evaluated 338 professional tasks spanning 30 to 110 actions. Gemini 3.1 Pro had the best Avg Pass—30.67% across 1,014 runs; Kimi K2.6 had the best Pass@3: 41.42% of tasks were solved in at least one of three independent attempts. These are measures from one benchmark, not a share of occupations ready for automation.

A typical failure began quietly. The agent skipped a stage or changed the wrong field, then acted quite reasonably in an already corrupted state. The error propagated, the nearest subtask displaced the original goal, and dozens of correct moves added up to the wrong job.

CoDA-Bench shows a neighboring failure. Across 1,009 tasks, an agent had to find data among an average of 980.8 files, write code, and run it. Mini-SWE-Agent with GPT-5.5 found the complete and only required file set in 83.0% of cases (DA), but produced the correct final answer in 61.1% (EA). On CoDA-Hard, Claude Code with Sonnet 4.6 rose from 45.4% to 73.1% EA when the prompt supplied exact file paths.

Both tests are artificial: Workflow-GYM removed login and authorization, a separate model grades part of the result, and CoDA-Bench is built from Kaggle-like environments. They did not test mandates. Their role is narrower and more important: to show why a technical trace of actions is not enough. The next question is whether that trace can prove whose assignment the agent was continuing.

Story 02

A Log Does Not Know Who Authorized It

A technical route tells us what triggered what. It does not necessarily tell us who authorized the work to continue. For an agent, that difference becomes a chasm: one task splits into repeated attempts and child operators, while several assignments pass through a single service.

In a preprint, Abhinav Mishra and Kumar Sharad show a simple limit. The same set of events and the same causal route can correspond to different distributions of authority if the link to the assignment was not recorded during execution. After an incident, it cannot be reconstructed honestly from timing or a session name.

The authors propose storing two graphs. An execution graph links calls and responses. An authority graph links the originating person or organization, the assignment, child agents, and the bounds of delegation. They have different lineages: an action may enter a new technical process while still spending old authority.

The preprint supports a narrower proposal: bind events during execution to a persistent delegation_id, principal, and agent, and bind child delegations to their parent. A full mandate would require more: purpose, permitted actions and resources, duration, a limit on further delegation, a means of revocation, and checks against those bounds before a material effect. The proposed CIM does not validate intent or policy and is not a prevention or revocation mechanism.

That same week, Atos and Microsoft put a scale on the future problem. Atos is rolling out Microsoft 365 Copilot to 56,000 employees, while Agent 365 is intended to manage a growing population of 19,000 agents: agents acting on behalf of people, agents with their own credentials, and agents arriving from the external ecosystem.

This is a joint Atos and Microsoft release, not independent verification of 19,000 active agents: it does not disclose active use, task volume, ROI, or quality. The preprint is not independent either—both authors are affiliated with Splunk/Cisco—and it is not peer reviewed; its evaluation uses synthetic data and a local deployment of roughly 70,000 events. Actions outside the gateway remain invisible.

But the new unit of management is already visible. Instead of a count of agents created, companies will need to show active mandates: owner, limits of authority, term, child assignments, and last irreversible action. If these fields do not appear in enterprise logs by mid-2027, the ‘mandate’ will remain a research schema rather than a new institution.

Chapter 02

Then, Separate the Role from the Operator

Story 03

The Workspace Gives an Agent Its Hands

If a mandate must be checked before action, who becomes the gatekeeper? In software development, the workspace is bidding for that role. In the Xcode 27 beta, Apple expanded built-in work with agents from Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI while preserving the option to connect other operators through the Agent Client Protocol.

Xcode is the common host. It shows the agent the code and active context and provides its own build, testing, Playgrounds, preview, and simulator tools. MCP is a separate channel for connecting external tools; ACP is for connecting compatible agents.

An open protocol therefore creates a double effect. It reduces dependence on any one lab while strengthening the host environment that gives competing models the same hands. Switching the operator becomes a setting; moving the state, tools, and rules becomes a migration.

In a separate planning mode, an agent can explore the project without changing code, while a person can discuss the plan before work begins. This is a useful boundary, but not a cryptographic mandate for the whole task: ordinary agent mode, command permissions, and folder access may be broader than a single assignment.

Xcode 27 is still a developer release, not a demonstrated productivity gain. ACP and MCP do not automatically transfer memory, policy, or quality; the strongest model may still produce a result unavailable to its peers. Apple may simply replace dependence on a model with dependence on a platform.

The distinguishing prediction is this: if model choice becomes a project setting within a year while state, permissions, and action history remain in the IDE, the owner of the workspace really has captured the mandate. If everything important continues to move with the model, this layer will remain a convenient shell.

Chapter 03

Finally, Set the Price and the Kill Switch

Story 04

A Mandate Becomes a Payment Credential

In payments, the metaphor ends. An agent either had the authority to spend money or it did not. That is why Visa plans to encode not only the identity of a digital buyer, but also the bounds of a specific assignment.

Visa and OpenAI announced that a user will be able to set spending limits, merchant categories, and required approvals. Under the plan, an agent will receive tokenized payment credentials and the network will check the transaction in real time. The ability to choose a product stays with the model; permission to pay stays with the payment layer.

Visa's broader announcement describes an Agentic Directory of verified agents and merchants, along with new signals in the payment token: who is paying, where the credential is being used, and how it behaved before. The payment network wants to become not only the road for money, but also the gatehouse for machine buyers.

The technical documentation draws an even more important boundary. A user confirms an instruction with a passkey; Visa then checks a request for credentials against that instruction, and at authorization checks the merchant and amount. After the purchase, the agent returns the result so a dispute can be tied to the original assignment.

A reliable agent signature does not provide this on its own. It answers who entered the store. A mandate answers whether the person authorized this purchase, for this amount, at this time. In the future, that distinction will anchor disputes: ‘the agent is authentic’ and ‘the payment was authorized’ are not the same claim.

Visa itself warns that the products are in development and deployment, may not be available everywhere, and have no disclosed volume of agent purchases. The Directory may remain a demonstration. It may also create a new rent: whoever decides which agents deserve trust gains the power to admit them to commerce.

The test is simple. If authorization and returns carry a separate agent-transaction marker, the bounds of the assignment, and proof of confirmation by mid-2027, the mandate has become a real payment object. If only a ‘trusted agent’ logo remains, this is a new badge, not a new institution.

Story 05

The Kill Switch Becomes Power Too

A mandate that cannot be revoked is not a mandate. But one shared kill switch creates a power capable of stopping legitimate work along with dangerous work.

On June 9, NIST brought to a public page an argument published earlier: no fixed, finite set of protective rules for prompts can be considered universally robust against every adaptive attack. The practical response is a continuous search for bypasses, updates, and the ability to contain damage and recover.

It is easy to turn that idea into the slogan ‘security is impossible.’ That is wrong. NIST's mathematical framework addresses a universal guarantee over an open prompt space; it does not prevent an external deterministic rule from reliably limiting a specific action—a transfer amount, a folder, a term, a machine. That is precisely why authority to act cannot remain inside the model's reasoning.

Three days later, the dispute became political. Anthropic said it received a US export directive barring access to Fable 5 and Mythos 5 by any foreign national—inside or outside the United States, including foreign-national Anthropic employees. The company said it disabled those two models for every customer; its other models were unaffected. AP reported the shutdown citing Anthropic; the directive itself was not made public.

According to Anthropic, the trigger was a narrow safeguard bypass used to find several previously known minor vulnerabilities; the company considered the response disproportionate. The Commerce Department did not comment to AP. It is therefore impossible to judge confidently either the technical threat or the quality of the government's decision.

The architectural lesson remains visible if the source caveat is preserved. According to Anthropic, the directive distinguished access by citizenship, while the company implemented it by shutting access for every customer. Centralized revocation reduces the risk of dangerous access, but turns an error or political conflict into an easy-to-trigger outage for innocent customers.

A mature mandate must be narrow, revocable, and portable. Task state lives outside the model; authority is checked before a material action; revocation blocks the future without erasing evidence of the past. If the hypothesis is right, by mid-2027 platforms will export not only task history, but the mandate owner, bounds, term, child assignments, and revocation status. Without that, a ‘replaceable agent’ remains a handsome button, while responsibility stays with the person who could not stop it.
