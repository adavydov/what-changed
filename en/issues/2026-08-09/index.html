Chapter 01

Where the machine gets an honest answer

Story 01

AI Hit the Cost of Truth

On August 4, Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 reported an unusual scale of autonomous vulnerability discovery. In two months, its NOVA system reviewed 3,915 open-source projects and produced 14,090 findings that passed its verification. Of those, 99.4% had not been publicly reported before, and the system rated about 40% high or critical severity.

The secret was not just the model. NOVA turned a suspected flaw into a working attack example, replayed it in a clean environment, and discarded a result that could not be reproduced. The agent received a short external answer: the vulnerability works or it does not. A human entered the loop only before the report went to the developer.

A day later, NormalTech showed the opposite result. The authors of two not-yet-published AI studies gave frontier agents the original question, six days, and thousands of dollars in compute. The agents completed the engineering work on their own, but the authors unambiguously rejected both resulting papers.

The machines narrowed the ambition too early, used less than half the budget, failed to backtrack from dead ends, and answered criticism with caveats instead of changing course. There was no test with a correct answer to run after every step. The agent had to decide which question deserved investigation in the first place and when the evidence was sufficient.

In drug discovery, this gap is more expensive. insitro founder Daphne Koller notes that more than 90% of drugs entering clinical trials fail. By her count, 38 popular targets each already have more than 50 programs, while the number of new targets entering the clinic each year fell from about 100 in 2015 to 30 in 2024. A molecule can be scored quickly; learning whether it helps a patient cannot.

The more precise rule is this: cheap verification is necessary, but it does not create autonomy by itself. Work accelerates when it can be split into reproducible steps, a local error can be seen before the end, and a failed move can be rolled back. Vulnerability discovery compiles well into such tests; choosing a research direction still does not.

A company's core AI asset is therefore larger than the model. It is a truth machine of control data, test environments, acceptance criteria, and escalation routes. Unit 42's numbers remain a security vendor's report, and NormalTech has only five runs around two papers. Yet together with biology, they show where to look for the next limit of automation: not in a list of occupations, but in the cost of reliable feedback inside each process.

Story 02

Weather Forecasting Gained a Thousand Futures

On August 6, Nature published the WeatherNext Cyclones paper. Google DeepMind's model produces global 15-day forecasts of a tropical cyclone's track, intensity, and size. On storms from 2023–2025, it provided at least a day of additional lead time on average over leading operational systems—an improvement comparable to the previous decade of forecasting progress.

The main change is not one precise line on a map. WeatherNext can create as many as 1,000 coherent atmospheric scenarios instead of the conventional 50. This makes it possible to see a rare but destructive path earlier and estimate not just the most likely outcome but the tail of risk.

Weather turned out to be a good example of a truth machine. The training set included nearly 20 terabytes of atmospheric history and an international archive of roughly 5,000 cyclones. Every past day can become an exam again: give the model the state before a storm and compare its forecast with what happened next.

The model operates on a global grid with cells about 28 kilometers wide—roughly a hundred times less detailed by area than specialist regional systems. This means some information about a cyclone's future intensity already exists in the large-scale structure of the atmosphere; it had not previously been extracted as effectively.

The stake is practical: a thousand fast scenarios can give a forecaster more warning time and give a power grid, port, or insurer a distribution of potential damage. But people retain the decision. The coauthors include experts from the US hurricane center and the UK Met Office, while the open repository explicitly directs users to government agencies for official warnings.

The evaluation still covers only three seasons, the full model requires powerful specialized chips, and the open lightweight version is less accurate. WeatherNext matters not as a promise of perfect weather, but as a demonstration of how quickly AI can advance in a complex physical system when it has long history, a strict score, and a way to test a thousand alternatives without living through each one.

Chapter 02

Who gets the right to change the system

Story 03

Services Firms Buy the Right to Change

On August 4, Multiplier raised $35M and disclosed a model unlike ordinary software sales to professional firms. The holding company has acquired eight accounting and advisory businesses, five of them in the past year, and signed terms for four more deals. A team of more than 30 technologists now works inside the acquired firms.

The firms keep their brands, leadership, and operating independence. Multiplier gains something else: continuous access to real workflows, data, professionals, and customer trust. The engineer sits next to the work, sees exceptions, and can change not only a product interface but the company's sequence of actions.

That is an important change in distribution. An outside SaaS vendor has code but little authority to rebuild a firm. A traditional owner has authority but often lacks a technology team. A permanent holding company combines both resources and expects to earn from improving the services themselves, not only from a subscription to a tool.

However, buying the firm is not required. Germany's HSP GRUPPE network of tax advisers remains an association of independent companies. In an OpenAI case study, 81 groups used ChatGPT, weekly activity reached 84%, and employees sent more than 500,000 messages over six months. The network supported adoption with common rules, learning formats, and reusable assistants.

HSP estimated a potential annual capacity gain of 40,000 hours and €3.8M, but that is a modeled scenario, not realized revenue. Multiplier likewise publishes no open comparison of quality, customer retention, or margins before and after acquisition. Both models currently show the structure of transformation better than its final economics.

The synthesis lies elsewhere: an organization's capacity to absorb technology is becoming scarce. It requires access to the flow of work, standards, reviewers, and authority to change the process over months. An acquisition buys that bundle immediately; a strong professional network can build it itself. A firm without this capacity risks becoming not the buyer of AI, but the distribution channel acquired by someone else's technology team.

Story 04

The Home Battery Became Part of the Grid

On August 3, Texas-based Base Power raised $1 billion at a $13 billion post-money valuation and unveiled Base Core, a modular 39.2-kilowatt-hour home battery. Two modules provide 78.4 kilowatt-hours, substantially more than typical backup systems. The company says it can install the base configuration in less than an hour.

A large battery is not only for outages. It charges when electricity and network capacity are plentiful and returns energy during the evening peak. A transmission line moves electricity through space; a battery moves it through time near the home.

Base says its fleet already exceeds 500 megawatt-hours and its utility agreements exceed 200 megawatts. If thousands of devices are managed together, the sale of home backup becomes a distributed power plant: the consumer gets resilience, while the grid gets capacity that can be activated at the needed hour.

The context explains the stake. Berkeley Lab counted about 8,200 active projects waiting to connect to the transmission grid at the end of 2025: 1,312 gigawatts of generation and 749 gigawatts of storage. Projects completed in 2025 had spent a median of more than five years in the queue; only 13% of capacity submitted from 2000–2020 had reached operation.

A home installation does not disappear from the permitting system and does not replace power lines. It needs a rate plan, interconnection, reliable communications, and a contract that lets an aggregator manage charging. Distributed hardware may be more expensive than a large plant, while the reported scale and installation speed still come from Base and its investors.

But the architectural shift is already visible. New capacity can be deployed not only as one large project outside the city, but as thousands of small nodes near demand. The energy company's advantage then includes not just the battery and capital, but the ability to find a home quickly, connect the device, and share the value fairly between the resident and the grid.

Chapter 03

How infrastructure separates from the human

Story 05

A Browser Is Designed Without a Human

On August 6, Cloudflare showed Kitesurf, a browser engine for agents that runs inside Workers. It has no tabs, themes, extensions, or other elements of a human interface. It does have JavaScript execution, web-standards compatibility, and structured output that is easier for a machine to read than screen pixels.

Each task gets a separate short-lived environment: open a page, perform an action, return data, and disappear. This runtime—an isolated execution container—does not retain a user profile between jobs. That reduces attack surface and avoids keeping Chromium permanently warm for an occasional request.

Cloudflare says the engine, just 12 weeks old, already passes more than 215,000 Web Platform Tests. In its internal experiment on 14 pages, Kitesurf used 3.1–3.8 times less CPU time and 4.7–7 times less memory than warm Chromium.

The cost of that efficiency is the speed of an individual task. In the same data, a page took 1.7–1.8 times longer to process. Each address had only five runs, and Cloudflare conducted the entire test itself. These numbers show the direction of optimization, but they do not yet provide a general ranking of browsers for agents.

Kitesurf does not yet support video, WebGL, extensions, long authenticated sessions, or some anti-bot protections. It therefore does not replace a familiar browser. It identifies a new class of work: a bounded task where isolation, cost, and machine-readable output matter more than perfect rendering for a person.

Agents used to receive a human computer and learn to click its buttons. Now web infrastructure is being rebuilt around their own form of consumption. If this layer grows, websites will have not only mobile and desktop performance, but a third kind: how cheaply, safely, and predictably a machine can execute them.
