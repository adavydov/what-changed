AI’s value depends on redesigning work and making results verifiable

The week’s main shift is from talking about what models can do to how processes are designed: who makes the decision, how the result is checked, what data the system can access, and where human control remains.

Published · 10–12 min read
Chapter 01

Main changes

Story 01

AI has an impact when it changes the process, not just speeds up a task

The focus is shifting from deploying a model to changing the roles, practices, and operating metrics of a specific process.

McKinsey warns that if an organization deploys advanced models while retaining the same management rules, fragmented data, technology environments, and role definitions, AI may improve individual tasks without changing the organization’s performance. The same logic appears in McKinsey’s observation: solutions are launched but do not become part of the work when roles, incentives, and daily practices remain unchanged.

That is why Deloitte identifies redesigning processes around agents, rather than layering agents onto the existing workflow, as the priority. Papaya Global offers a practical criterion: it links AI’s value to metrics from specific workflows, including error rate, flow ratios, and screen time.

Story 02

Autonomy does not eliminate the decision owner

The conversation around agents is increasingly tied to action thresholds, exception handling, and the observability of outcomes.

In the “AI as adviser” model, the system recommends and a human makes the decision; BCG separately stresses that the CEO retains responsibility for that decision. In the “AI as co-worker” model, the system acts within agreed thresholds and policies, while a human reviews results and exceptions.

Control is not limited to data storage: McKinsey also includes embeddings, prompts, memory layers, and generated outputs. Deloitte proposes tracking outcomes and metrics to confirm that agents are working as intended.

Story 03

A confident answer is not a signal that it is correct

Result verification and evaluation methodology are becoming independent constraints on scaling AI.

Accounting Today puts the problem bluntly: AI breaks the link between an answer’s confidence and its correctness. That makes it hard to find the places that need checking precisely when the output looks good even where it is wrong.

That checking itself becomes work: at production scale, successful deployment may, in the Accounting Today author’s view, cost as much as the work it displaced. The evaluation of GPT-5.6 at METR shows another side of the problem: a conclusion about a model can change radically depending on how evaluators account for deceptive behavior.

Story 04

Fake documents are becoming more convincing, and controls matter more

Signals from the AppZen platform and an Accounting Today review point to AI playing a larger role in fake receipts; this signal concerns flags on a specific corporate platform.

Accounting Today reports that AI-generated fake documents have become so sophisticated that even trained professionals struggle to distinguish genuine from fake. The article describes expense fraud as a scheme involving many transactions and small amounts, deliberately chosen to fall under automatic-approval thresholds.

The AppZen signal has narrow coverage: it is the share of flags for allegedly fraudulent receipts on its corporate platform, not an estimate of all expenses or all companies. That is why automatically approving primary documents without additional grounds for review is becoming an increasingly insufficient control layer.

Story 05

In tax practice, AI remains a starting point, not finished work

OPR clarified how tax practitioners’ existing obligations apply to work with AI.

The IRS Office of Professional Responsibility clarified that the existing Treasury Circular 230 rules apply to tax practitioners’ use of AI, and that duties of due diligence, competence, and confidentiality have not changed. According to OPR’s clarification, the tax practitioner remains fully responsible for work prepared with AI assistance.

OPR calls AI-generated content a starting point, not a finished product; it cannot be relied on alone. The office also requires using only secure, organization-approved AI for client data and understanding possible sources of error or bias in the tools used.

Additional signals

Story 06

Agents are becoming an execution layer, not a separate interface

Vendors describe agentic systems as a layer that coordinates actions among people, enterprise platforms, and other agents.

In Deloitte’s description, users, processes, and enterprise platforms, including ERP and CRM, interact with a multi-agent system to initiate or continue actions. The Omnia network is intended to bring new and existing AI agents together in a single structure so they can jointly coordinate and execute workflows.

In a procurement scenario, intelligent orchestration assesses suppliers on cost, availability, requirements compliance, and risk in order to initiate compliant purchasing decisions. The deeper an agent enters enterprise infrastructure and gains access to procurement systems, supplier relationships, and commercial data, the more important security architecture becomes.
