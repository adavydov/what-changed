Chapter 01

Product and production

Story 01

Papaya tripled the number of workers served by the same team

Papaya Global put AI into a paid add-on. Enterprise customers rejected it because they already saw those capabilities as part of the product. The team tied each feature to a payroll bottleneck and a measurable change in the process.

Priorities came from a table that placed operating failures beside relevant model capabilities. Papaya sees few repeated events, while one mistake can be expensive. There are no millions of identical actions to learn from, so engineers work through rare exceptions with payroll specialists.

Matching a payslip to an employee initially worked in only 30% of cases. Transliteration changed names, document formats varied, and a manager's name could appear beside the employee's. The rate climbed to nearly 85% after redesign. The 99% target would send almost the entire flow through automation, with people handling disputed documents.

Fourteen capabilities went into existing plans. After the early deployments, Papaya served three times as many workers with the same internal headcount. AI is measured here through the amount of work that can pass through the company.

Each capability has its own numbers. The team watches errors and the share of documents cleared without manual review. Screen time exposes friction. Revenue and workers served per employee connect the process change to economics.

Support directs a customer to self-service when the capability already exists. Webinars and campaigns reach teams that still follow the old routine. Adoption becomes part of daily customer service.

Every feature carries two feedback buttons. The compliance agent works from a rule base spanning more than 160 countries and sends uncertain cases to an internal expert. The correction returns to the knowledge base. Papaya hired a vice president of AI after the first working version had already proved its value.

Story 02

Every index needs an expiry

At one financial company, a single PDF now produces text, tables, and images. Summaries, metadata, sensitivity labels, and quality scores follow. Each new object outlives the original request and can enter the model's next answer.

The company built a shared, extensible pipeline. Ingestion and extraction happen once. Quality checks, metadata, lineage, indexing, and delivery are available to new use cases without another build. McKinsey estimated $10 million to $20 million in potential cost avoidance as use cases multiply.

Every derived object gets a record. Each index has an owner, a version, and a refresh schedule. The same record holds its audit history and retirement date. An old index can no longer live silently beside a new policy.

A document update moves predictably into embeddings and search indexes. The previous version receives an explicit status. The system can see which answers and processes relied on it.

Access rules continue to run while the model works. They check retrieved passages, the model request, and memory. The same policy reaches the finished output before display or a write into the accounting system.

For an accounting product, this lifecycle begins when a document arrives. Its record keeps the source version, extraction quality, and the index that supplied a passage. When the source changes, the operator gets the related objects that require refresh.

Story 03

A fake receipt now targets the approval threshold

An image generator can produce a fresh receipt in minutes. A small amount slips below the manual review threshold, and the next document gets a different design. Controls built for rare, repeated fakes begin to miss a stream of one-offs.

In AppZen data, AI-generated documents rose from 0% of receipts already marked suspicious in March 2025 to 70.8% by mid-May 2026. The Forbes sample contained 1,471 such receipts from 745 employees at 174 companies. Claimed reimbursement totaled $148,143.

A small receipt makes the attack convenient. It is less likely to reach a person, while an employee can spread the amount across weeks and categories. A fixed cash threshold sees each document alone.

AppZen starts with the file's digital fingerprint and metadata. The system then verifies the merchant against catalogs and searches for evidence of a real business. Arithmetic, required fields, and document format contribute separate signals.

Repetition emerges across the organization. The same template may appear under different employees months apart. A reviewer's decision stays in the history, giving the next similar case a revised risk level.

An accounting product can link the receipt to a bank transaction, merchant, and purchase time. A business trip adds itinerary and participants. The reviewer sees disagreement among independent traces of the event, and the decision enriches the next review.

Story 04

AI makes a tax firm prove every conclusion and every billed hour

On June 24, the IRS Office of Professional Responsibility issued Issue 2026-19 on AI in tax practice. It translates Circular 230 into concrete actions inside a firm.

Before work reaches a client or the IRS, a practitioner checks facts, citations, and calculations produced by the model. The practitioner's name remains attached to the result. A model error becomes the professional's error once it passes through review.

Written advice requires reading every cited case and rule. Forecasts must be checked against inputs, and formulas must be recalculated. When the system's logic is opaque to the practitioner, reliance on its answer may be unreasonable under Circular 230.

A practitioner must understand how the tool forms an answer, where its limits sit, and what risks arise from client data. Confidential information travels only through enterprise-approved systems. The upload route to an unapproved public service is closed.

Firm policy covers staff training and secure data handling. It defines third-party vendor review. Accuracy is monitored after launch, and each step of the work and review remains documented.

OPR addresses price directly. Billing for manual hours saved by AI may constitute an unconscionable fee. Double billing for the person and the model creates the same risk. Savings are openly credited to the client, and AI activity is disclosed when the nature of the engagement calls for it.

A tax-firm platform can assemble an evidence packet with every answer. It contains source versions and recalculated formulas. The log identifies the reviewer, approval time, and the system through which client data traveled.

Story 05

A B2B agent needs a commitment log

Sellers and buyers already see the same B2B deal differently. Among sellers, 72% call sales mostly automated. Only 47% of buyers agree. A buyer is six times more likely than a seller to describe the process as mainly manual.

The gap reaches spending. Deloitte found that buyers with a great experience spend nearly 30% more. An agent enters a process where the seller's internal systems may already feel automated while the customer still carries data between screens and waits for people to respond.

The market is only beginning to run autonomous agents. Eleven percent of companies have agents in production. Another 42% are developing a strategy, and 35% have none. About 20% of organizations have mature governance for autonomous agents.

An agent needs current data in a machine-readable form. A product record connects to price and inventory. Customer and vendor policies define the terms available in a deal. A policy change reaches the next offer immediately.

Authority is divided into bounded actions. An agent may quote inside an approved range, reserve inventory, or request approval. Every promise enters a log with its author, basis, and expiry.
