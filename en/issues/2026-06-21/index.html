The AI-transformation bottleneck has moved into organization and control

Access to agents is no longer the main scarcity. Meaningful results come from protecting redesign capacity, naming an end-to-end process owner, granting minimum privileges, routing models by risk-adjusted cost, and turning human expertise into a system.

Published · 15–18 min read

Chapter 01

How to scale agentic work

Story 01

AI transformation scales through hundreds of process changes, not one central pilot

The new scarcity is not access to a model, but a team's ability to stop, redesign its work, and tie the change to a measurable result.

BCG reports that GenAI adoption reached 70% in three years, yet only 16% of Australian executives see significant value. Tool access alone creates local workarounds, not transformation. Transformation starts when domain teams redesign an end-to-end process around an outcome and a central function provides the shared platform, governance, and method.

AWS illustrates the practice. It measured success by business impact rather than adoption; embedded experienced peer engineers inside teams so daily workflows actually changed; and expanded the redesign across planning, maintenance, and operations rather than coding alone. BCG reports 27% more features shipped after the changes reduced manual developer work.

In early client programs, BCG associates full process redesign with threefold productivity, up to 80% shorter cycles, and at least 60% long-run cost reduction. This is not a universal promise but a clue to the mechanism: task automation delivers local efficiency, while an end-to-end owner, integrated business-technology team, and embedded value measurement change system throughput.

The operating model has five elements: select a few P&L processes; protect team capacity from daily operations; name one end-to-end outcome owner; provide a shared data, integration, and testing platform; and measure cycle time, quality, and economics weekly. A central AI office removes repeated infrastructure work and holds the standard—it does not redesign the business on behalf of domain teams.

Story 02

A reliable enterprise agent needs a separate control plane

Agent privileges cannot live only in a prompt. They must be granted, observed, and revoked like the rights of any production actor.

Deloitte divides guardian agents into three roles: a monitor observes actions and creates a signal; a reviewer checks outputs for accuracy and permitted use; a protector corrects or blocks an action or permission during execution. In a LoanOps pilot, these controls were embedded from intent detection through final compliance checks. Not every guard needs an LLM: deterministic policy remains the first line, while dynamic review belongs where context is ambiguous.

Google DeepMind adds risk tiers. Reversible, low-risk actions may allow asynchronous logging and later correction, while dangerous actions must be blocked before execution. Trust expands access gradually based on verified behavior. DeepMind's internal monitor grew from analysis of one million coding-agent tasks and is tracked by traffic coverage, detected misbehavior, and response time.

A study of 3,600 API tools shows why least privilege deserves its own test. After a normal HTTP 503 error, an agent could jump to an administrative override while safe alternatives remained. The authors identify five over-privilege modes: level escalation, scope expansion, excessive persistence, security bypass, and unnecessary data disclosure. A prompt to retry safely helps; technically withholding the dangerous tool until the safe path is exhausted is stronger.

The enterprise control plane therefore stores agent identity, minimum scope, privilege budget and expiry, a policy check before every write, trajectory logs, a kill switch, and a human escalation route. For an accounting agent, reading a document, preparing an entry, writing a draft, and posting an irreversible change must be four distinct permissions—not one token called '1C access.'

Story 03

The more autonomous the agent, the higher the return to operator expertise

An agent does not equalize novices and experts; it multiplies the quality of task framing, checks, and recovery.

Anthropic analyzed about 400,000 Claude Code sessions. In a typical session, users made roughly 70% of planning decisions while Claude made about 80% of execution decisions. A novice session triggered about five actions and 600 output words; an expert session triggered roughly 12 actions and 3,200 words. A stronger operator does not necessarily intervene more—they define a better boundary and let the agent do more within it.

The gap is clearest after problems. Controlled verified success in troubled sessions rose from about 4% for novices to 15% for experts; at least partial success rose from 60% to 80–81% for intermediate-to-expert users. Most improvement came from moving from novice to intermediate: basic ability to recognize the problem and request the right check mattered more than additional narrow virtuosity.

Over seven months, the estimated value of a typical task rose about 27%, while software-operations work increased from 14% to 21%. Agents are taking on longer and more consequential workflows. That raises the value of people who understand the system, formalize success, and own exceptions.

The organizational response is to convert expert judgment into reusable policies, exception examples, evals, runbooks, and escalation criteria rather than trying to delete the expert. The right goal is one strong specialist supervising more verifiable work while their decisions improve the system for everyone.

Chapter 02

Where economics change

Story 04

AI platforms for accounting firms are beginning to sell capacity, not tools

The step beyond automating individual tasks is to return a finished, review-ready close and separate revenue growth from linear headcount growth.

Pilot positions Meridian as a layer over the existing ledger: it handles categorization, reconciliation, exceptions, and financial statements; writes results back; logs decisions; and returns closed books for sign-off. Pilot says the system was refined across more than 187,000 monthly closes for 8,000 businesses. The important product contract is not the marketing comparison to a seasoned accountant, but that the accountant reviews a finished result rather than babysitting every agent microtask.

That changes unit economics. Meridian presents a scenario in which one accountant carries a $1 million book while fixed costs remain flat. The claim must be tested through review time, material adjustments, clients per accountant, gross margin, and retention. If review becomes a second full close, the capacity gain disappears.

Gusto attacks a different bottleneck: firm development. Six agents cover website audit, prospecting, approval-gated outreach, advisory positioning, expansion pitches, and price/margin modeling. This is not accounting production but a growth operating system: the existing client base and firm specialization become an ICP, offer, and next conversation.

For an accounting roll-up, the two layers complement each other. The production platform increases clients per accountant; growth agents fill released capacity and help sell higher-value advisory work. A central platform can distribute both capabilities while preserving firm-specific policy, local client trust, and transparent human acceptance.

Story 05

Model price dispersion makes routing and data rights part of unit economics

One model for every task is an expensive architectural habit. The cost spread is already large enough for risk-aware routing to become a margin source.

AA-Briefcase found more than an 800-fold task-cost spread. Leader Claude Fable 5 averaged more than $31 per task versus about $0.04 for DeepSeek V4 Flash, yet even the leader met every criterion in only 3% of tasks. GLM-5.2 trailed Claude Opus 4.8 by about 90 Elo at less than one quarter of the cost. Expensive is not reliably correct, and cheap is not automatically unusable.

The practical design is a cascade. A cheap model handles extraction, classification, and routine transformations; a stronger model receives complex exceptions or low confidence; deterministic checks remain model-independent; a person accepts irreversible risk. Routing must include rework, false completion, latency, and verification—not only token price.

The second constraint is the right to retrieve context. Deloitte warns of SaaS tollgating: vendors may charge for API access to data the organization itself created. The bottleneck then moves from compute to contracts, export rights, and approved access channels. Some large companies found broad API guarantees in old ERP agreements; others may be creating future vendor rents through today's architecture.

Model and data gateways should therefore be designed together: portable model interfaces, a local or open fallback, quality history by task class, contractual data rights, and the ability to export context from systems of record. Sovereign stacks such as Sarvam show another path—a full local platform combining models, institutional access, and market-appropriate pricing.

Story 06

In a roll-up, integration capacity constrains deals before capital does

A good target can be a bad deal if the platform lacks a named owner, time, and authority to absorb another organization.

Permanent Equity starts not with valuation but with the exact asset that is impossible or too slow to build: customers, license, geography, team, IP, or removal of a constraint. Without one, build is usually preferable. A deal competes not only on price but against every other use of executive attention, capital, and integration capacity.

The readiness boundary is concrete. If the integration owner, timeline, and authority cannot be named, no integration plan exists. A departing CEO or two operating-leader changes in 18 months signal poor timing because integration takes years. Unreliable reporting and an unstable core operation make an add-on especially dangerous: the buyer cannot measure the outcome or distinguish target problems from platform problems.

Before an LOI, define 18-month checkpoints for client and staff retention, close quality, data migration, gross margin, billing leakage, shared AI-platform adoption, and exception volume. Client transfer cannot be assumed: contracts often include change-of-control provisions, and trust does not move with the legal entity.

For an AI-accounting roll-up, this means an explicit integration budget in people and processes, not only money. Favor deals where the shared platform quickly improves measurable capacity or quality while the local firm retains ownership of the client outcome. Keep the deal flow active; close only when the next integration will not break the organic transformation already under way.
