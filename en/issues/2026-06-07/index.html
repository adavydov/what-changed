Issue 01 · June 1–7, 2026

Cheap AI leaves the money in products built around the model

Alex Imas and Phil Trammell show where the gains from cheap machine intelligence may accrue. Founders need an application that knows the customer's context and owns the completed work.

Published · About 10 min read
Chapter 01

Where value remains

Story 01

AI may become a cheap utility like electricity

In a conversation with Dwarkesh Patel, economists Alex Imas and Phil Trammell examine a world where machine intelligence follows the distributed economics of electricity. Imas develops the idea in his essay What will be scarce?

Media collect rent around network size. Advertisers go where the audience already is. In electricity markets, large suppliers generate power while factories, shops, and services connected to the grid capture an enormous share of the gains. Language models have yet to show a durable loop that permanently binds demand to one provider.

Labs control compute, scarce talent, private data, and streams of user feedback. These resources accelerate experiments and lower training cost. A network effect demands more: each new customer must make the product more valuable to the next customer. If open models catch the leader within six to nine months, exclusive access creates a short window of high margins.

Open models change a buyer's negotiating position well before they reach full parity with the leader. Most processes judge a model by its accuracy on a narrow set of operations. The full range of capability sits outside the customer outcome. Once a second model passes the company's own tests, the option to switch creates bargaining power. The frontier provider keeps the hardest cases. High-volume work moves to a cheaper layer.

This changes product pricing for founders. The markup on model access will shrink with the price of machine intelligence. Customers will pay for a closed month, a resolved exception, an accepted application, or a detected error. That unit of price includes context, tools, control, and responsibility. The product can change models inside it and reserve expensive intelligence for difficult cases.

This issue bets on the electricity path. A practical test takes one week: the team switches the primary model interface and runs its private task suite. In AI accounting, the suite covers a routine close, rare documents, discrepancies, and recovery after an error. Decision history, permissions, feedback, and result quality should survive the supplier change.

Story 02

Human time gets more expensive where responsibility matters

Imas calls the relational sector the set of services where human participation forms part of the result's value. A doctor may hand document collection, calls, and forms to a machine. Patients may keep paying to discuss a diagnosis with a person. Conjoint analysis can measure that willingness to pay by presenting several versions of the same service and changing human participation at one specific point.

Imas uses Starbucks as an example. The company spent years mechanizing coffee preparation and service in pursuit of thin margins. Management later brought back more baristas, handwritten notes on cups, ceramic mugs, and places where customers could sit. A standardized drink is easy to reproduce. Time, attention, and hospitality stay scarce enough for customers to notice them inside a mass-market chain.

Research by David Autor and Neil Thompson gives occupations a sharper frame. When technology takes the simpler tasks, the remaining work demands rarer expertise. Specialist pay rises and fewer people clear the entry threshold. Automating a difficult task can open the occupation to a wider group. In accounting, the outcome depends on which part of expertise the system absorbs and what it teaches the employee preparing for the next level.

The technical boundary follows the cost of error. In an O-ring process, one weak step can spoil the whole result. After nine operations are automated, the tenth manual operation can easily become a queue. The team needs a map of decisions showing where a rule is enough, where a model should assess context, and where a person must recognize a rare exception and accept responsibility.

In AI accounting, the machine collects source documents, matches records, performs calculations, and prepares evidence. The accountant receives disputed items with the relevant context already assembled. Each exception type has a risk threshold, a required document, and a named person with signing authority. Client conversations remain attached to decisions that change taxes, reporting, or company obligations.

The exception queue becomes the heart of the product. Each card shows the source document, affected entries, applied rule, reason for doubt, and decision deadline. The accountant accepts an option, corrects it, or asks the client for more information. The system stores the choice and its reason. The next similar case receives a sharper route, while the disputed decision remains available for inspection.

A manager can see review hours by exception type, return rates, and cost per accepted operation. Those measures show where to add a rule, improve context, or preserve personal work as part of the service's value.

Chapter 02

What to build around the model

Story 03

One early error can derail an agent's entire task

ToolMaze tests failures along two axes. An explicit error returns a 404 or timeout. A hidden error arrives in a valid structure with corrupted meaning. A transient failure disappears after a retry. A permanent one requires another path or a stopped process.

Hidden errors proved the most dangerous. Agents trusted plausible tool responses and carried the poisoned value forward. Recovery rates fell by roughly 37% in those scenarios. General task execution improved with model scale 3.66 times faster than fault tolerance. Scale gave the agent more power to travel along its chosen path, including a path corrupted by an early value.

K-BrowseComp shows what happens after retrieval succeeds. A model finds part of the evidence, chooses a plausible candidate too early, and starts collecting support around it. Another failure appears across parallel branches: the constraints are present, yet the link among them disappears. GPT-5.5 made an average of 9.30 search calls in failed trials and 7.08 in successful ones. Extra search produced more material for the wrong candidate.

The workflow becomes an observable state system. The agent saves a checkpoint before each critical transition. A candidate-and-constraint table stores the source, freshness marker, entity role, and verification status. The next stage opens after required constraints are closed. Tests deliberately make external tools fail. An irreversible action passes through a separate permission gate.

The production record separates business state from attempt state. Confirmed facts live in the first layer: a document was posted, an invoice was paid, or a period was closed. Model calls, retries, and intermediate hypotheses live in the second. After a failure, the agent reloads the confirmed state. An idempotency key protects the external system from a second payment or a duplicate entry.

HarnessForge changes planning, action, and memory structures together with the reasoning policy. Joint adaptation beat the strongest separate approaches across five benchmarks by 3.56% on average. The final matched pair scored 77.19% on API-Bank. The same harness with earlier policies averaged 71.93%.

A pilot tracks correct completion, false completion, recovery after failure, and escalation to a person. The last measure includes the quality of the package handed to the specialist: source data, action trace, reason for stopping, and the proposed next step. Recovery cost and unnecessary calls after the first error are recorded separately.

Story 04

A financial agent finds the path, strict rules do the calculation

In XBRL, every reported figure is linked to an accounting concept, period, unit, and context. An auditor follows those connections, finds related facts, reconstructs the calculation, and applies the rule. The audit trail preserves that path for another specialist to replay.

AuditFlow maintains two graphs. A static graph contains the US GAAP taxonomy with definitions and calculation relationships. A dynamic graph is built from the specific filing: facts, periods, units, and dimensions. A bridge between them leads from a number in the document to the rule that constrains it.

Two junior agents follow different routes. The first starts with taxonomy requirements and searches for the required facts. The second starts with filing evidence, compares periods, and inspects arithmetic relationships. A senior agent diagnoses a disagreement and sends them back for a specific missing piece of evidence. The final report contains a verdict, reported and expected values, the action path, supporting evidence, and a trust score.

Every junior passes through a required-tool gate. The system runs checks for sign, calculation tree, and dimensional sums. The second route also retrieves the fact's history. An early attempt to finish returns the list of missing actions. The language model chooses the next area to inspect. The result of a strict operation grounds the verdict.

The ablation exposes the load-bearing role of the architecture. On 67 cases, removing deterministic checks reduced joint accuracy from 82.09% to 17.91%, increased invalid results from 1.49% to 35.82%, and eliminated accuracy on two DQC rules. Removing the mandatory-tool gate also hurt performance: joint accuracy fell to 74.63% and invalid outputs rose to 17.91%.

Before posting, the system assembles an operation package: proposed entries, links to source-document fields, the rule version, control results, and open exceptions. The gate checks completeness and authority. A person receives one connected object. The decision joins the same package with a name and timestamp. Deterministic tools execute arithmetic, checksums, accounting-system rules, and regulatory tests.

Story 05

Context turns a model into a working system

An agent needs the exact state of the company at the moment of a decision. A larger pile of documents can bury the required fact under obsolete versions.

In a No Priors conversation, Satya Nadella describes the enterprise harness through models, data, and tools. A feedback loop runs across them. The company collects private quality tests built around familiar work because public benchmarks saturate quickly and rarely match the company's own value. Context is prepared for the agent's current plan and disclosed as the work requires it.

This layer extends beyond RAG. Entities across CRM, accounting systems, messages, and documents are linked. Every field receives a source of truth. Permissions travel with the data. The system tracks freshness, writes results back, and captures feedback. A raw document dump forces the model to guess or ask a person to reconstruct the company inside one prompt.

The Google Cloud case shows the scale. Preparing inbound leads once ran in batches and took almost a week. A multi-agent system built on Vertex AI, BigQuery, and Gemini processed 25,000 records in four hours, roughly 30 times faster. It brings internal records, external signals, and refreshed datasets directly into the sales process.

Control changed as quality stabilized. People initially reviewed results directly and compared the selected contact with human work. The team later moved to risk-based sampling. Regions and scenarios with wider variation receive more scrutiny. Edge cases go to a dedicated group, while audit loops catch retries and timeouts. The source reports a reduction in cost to serve of up to 80%.

For AI founders, the context layer may outlast the model itself. An entity graph, permissions, change history, read and write interfaces, and an evaluation set built from real exceptions are hard to move to a competitor. A document archive remains raw material. The asset grows from decisions: which fact was accepted, which exception went to a person, which result returned to the system of record, and which result passed the next control.

Chapter 03

A roll-up operating model

Story 06

How 22 people at headquarters oversee 85 companies

Lagercrantz owns 85 companies. Roughly 22 people work at headquarters. That ratio rests on a clear boundary: the center manages capital and transparency, while operating decisions stay close to the customer.

The group buys healthy niche B2B companies that remain owner-led. Targets typically have EBIT margins of 15–20% or more. The episode describes price discipline at 4–8 times EBITDA even when private equity offers around 11 times. Acquisitions are funded from internally generated cash flow, and the acquired companies stay in the group permanently.

The deal model preserves brands, local leadership, and operating methods. Small divisional teams support company leaders. Country leads appeared as the footprint expanded. The center sets principles and capital-allocation criteria. This structure reserves headquarters attention for decisions that affect the whole portfolio.

Accountability for a deal continues after closing. The employee who led an acquisition will often join the acquired company's board and remain tied to its outcome. Material deals pass several discussions and the board. The group tracks growth in earnings and cash flow per share, buys stable cash flow, and accepts that with 10–12 deals a year roughly 10% may disappoint. Incremental changes proceed without a mandatory 100-day plan.

For an accounting roll-up, the boundary sits between the shared platform and the client decision. The center holds capital allocation, quality standards, shared data, AI infrastructure, and performance visibility. The local firm keeps responsibility for the client. Every shared measure has one definition, one source, and a refresh cadence. Every exception has a local owner who knows the contract and the history of the relationship.

AI gives a small headquarters a current view of the portfolio. The system sees a delayed month-end close, rising manual corrections, or a repeated error before the quarterly report. A proven practice travels between companies with its author, original situation, and scope attached. The client decision stays with the firm that will explain it and live with the consequences.
