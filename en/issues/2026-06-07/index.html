Chapter 01

Where the money goes

Story 01

AI as electricity: products around the model capture the money

On Dwarkesh Patel’s podcast, economists Alex Imas and Phil Trammell start with a clean question: who captures the benefit when machine intelligence becomes cheap? Two images map most of the market. AI could resemble media or electricity.

Media gravitates toward a center because network size is part of the product. Advertisers choose the platform with the largest audience. People join because other people are already there. Each participant helps sell the next one. A few platforms can collect rent from an entire market.

Electricity spread the benefit differently. The utility sold kilowatt-hours while factories, shops, and households created most of the economic value. Power grids changed how plants were organized and enabled whole categories of appliances. Thousands of downstream products turned the common input into useful work.

Frontier labs have a real lead. They control compute, attract scarce researchers, and receive feedback from heavy use. Those assets accelerate the next model. A durable monopoly needs another mechanism: each customer must make the product permanently more valuable to the next customer. Language models have yet to show that loop.

A lab can still become an enormous business by selling scarce intelligence when demand is intense. The conversation with Dwarkesh asks a larger question. Can one supplier collect a share from every industry using AI for years? A temporary capability lead and a large budget do not create that right. Customers need to lose accumulated value when they leave.

Open-weight models lag the frontier by roughly six to nine months in the scenario discussed by Imas and Trammell. That window lets the leader charge for the hardest work. Most buyers need high accuracy on a narrow set of operations. A second model becomes a substitute when it passes the company’s own tests.

A simple swap test reveals who owns the product. Replace the primary model and run real customer cases. The model behaves like an electricity supplier when quality holds and the history of decisions stays intact. If the service collapses, its moat lives inside someone else’s interface.

Pricing moves closer to the completed result. A buyer understands a closed month, an accepted claim, a detected error, or a resolved exception. The product can route rare cases to an expensive model and send routine work through a cheaper layer. Margin comes from knowing the process and finishing the job.

For AI accounting, the owned asset starts with live tests: an ordinary close, an unusual document, a mismatch between systems, and recovery after a failure. Decision history and exception routes come next. The model vendor can change in days while the accumulated work remains inside the product.

Story 02

An industry can grow while the profession inside it shrinks

Automation sends an industry and its workers along different paths. A market may grow after production costs fall sharply. The number of professionals inside it may collapse at the same time. This split is more useful than asking whether AI will take jobs in general.

Imas explains the mechanism through a product made of ten linked tasks. A machine takes nine and a person keeps the last one. The product gets cheaper. If demand responds strongly enough, companies produce far more of it and may hire more people for the remaining task. This is what happens when a market remains far from saturation.

Agriculture eventually met a physical ceiling. A person can consume only so much food. The industry’s share of US GDP fell from roughly 8% to 0.7% over seventy years. Productivity kept rising after demand had stopped keeping pace.

Textiles followed a different path in the nineteenth century. Automation cut the price of cloth by about fifty times. Consumption rose one hundredfold and the number of weavers increased fourfold. Employment contracted much later, after demand had begun to saturate.

Software, legal help, and some accounting services have an enormous reservoir of unmet demand. Millions of smaller companies operate without management accounts, cash forecasts, or quick advice on a complicated transaction. A lower price opens this work to new customers and gives the industry room to grow.

You can look for elasticity before a major automation program. Break the service into concrete outcomes, then observe demand at a lower price and a shorter delivery time. How many companies would order a weekly cash forecast for the first time? How often would an existing client review its books if the review cost one tenth as much? That curve says more about the future market than current industry revenue.

Market growth makes no promise to a profession. One product can serve thousands of new customers with a small team. Human participation remains valuable where the person is part of the service itself. Imas calls this the relational sector. A patient buys a conversation with a doctor. A collector feels a connection with an artist. A client trusts the signature of a professional who knows the situation.

An art experiment makes the mechanism tangible. Buyers valued a print made by a person much more highly than an AI-made version. When they learned that five hundred identical copies existed, the price of the human work fell. The connection to the artist had weakened. The AI version barely moved because buyers already treated it as an impersonal commodity.

The product opportunity is precise. The machine expands access, assembles evidence, and prepares the decision. Human time sits at the moment where a customer pays for judgment, trust, or accepted responsibility. That moment may take ten minutes at the end of a long automated process and still carry the value of the whole service.

In accounting, that moment could be a disputed entry, a conversation about tax exposure, an exception decision, or a legally meaningful sign-off. The review card brings the source document, affected accounts, applicable rule, and reason for doubt. The customer receives a decision from someone who can see the whole case after the inexpensive work is complete.

Chapter 02

What makes an AI product defensible

Story 03

A company’s own evaluations become the core AI product asset

Satya Nadella offers a hard ownership test for an AI product. Swap model A for model B and watch the company’s own task set. When the score improves without rebuilding the system, the company controls the product. When accumulated quality disappears with the model, the supplier owns the main asset.

A private evaluation starts with real work. It contains successful cases, expensive failures, rare exceptions, and decisions that made specialists disagree. Each example stores the input, expected result, and the reason that result counts as good. A public benchmark measures general model ability. This set measures the ability to finish a particular customer’s job.

A good evaluation ends in the system where the result lives. The email reaches the right person, the journal entry passes control, the contract receives the required change. Scoring polished prose says little about this work. The team runs the same task on the old and new versions, then compares the final outcome and the cost of corrections.

The No Priors conversation describes a climb from a general model to a specialist. The team wraps the model in a workflow, studies failures, changes tools and instructions, and reruns the same tasks. Work traces become training material. Over time, the agent learns ways of acting that resemble the experience of a long-tenured employee.

A useful trace is smaller than a full conversation log. It connects the situation, selected action, specialist correction, and result written into the operating system. It shows which document mattered, why an exception fired, and where the answer went. This record moves between models and survives a change in interface.

Legora builds this protection inside legal work. The product gains access to a firm’s proprietary data, enters the daily workflow, and remembers how users teach it to handle a particular matter. General legal information is widely available. Accumulated team behavior is much harder to reproduce.

Sensei reaches the same point in another product. Value grows from user behavior and feedback: which suggestion was accepted, edited, or discarded. A competitor may have an equally capable model. Decision history keeps improving the product for its own audience.

Google Cloud shows the engineering payoff. Preparing 25,000 inbound leads used to take almost a week. A system built with Vertex AI, BigQuery, and Gemini completed the work in four hours. It pulled internal data and fresh external signals into the sales process. Once performance stabilized, the team moved from universal human review to risk-based samples.

An accounting product accumulates this asset around exceptions. It stores the source document, rule version, specialist correction, and final journal entry. A familiar case follows an established route. A rare one goes to a person and joins the evaluation set. After several months, the company owns a library of work that can test any new model.

Chapter 03

How the work reaches a correct result

Story 04

Agents are learning to finish tasks faster than they learn to recover

A stronger model gets further through a long task. An early mistake travels further too. The agent may take ten more confident steps, write a corrupted value, and send out a plausible-looking result.

ToolMaze tests four situations. A tool may return an obvious failure such as a timeout. Sometimes the response has the expected shape while carrying a false value. A temporary problem clears after a retry. A persistent one requires another route or a stop.

Hidden failures were the most dangerous. Agents accepted a tidy response and carried the error forward. Successful recovery in these scenarios fell by about 37%. General task ability grew with model scale 3.66 times faster than the ability to survive a tool failure.

Each failure calls for a different response. A timeout may permit a retry. A corrupted value requires rereading the source and undoing every action built on it. A persistent outage sends the task down a backup route. One universal instruction to try again mixes these situations and lets the agent repeat the same damage with more confidence.

K-BrowseComp catches a similar trap in web research. The agent picks a plausible candidate too early and gathers supporting material around it. Failed GPT-5.5 attempts made 9.30 search calls on average, compared with 7.08 for successful attempts. Extra work often strengthened the first wrong theory.

The Emergent team encountered the state problem while running agents in parallel and built disk and memory snapshots. Every worker can begin from the same state. A failed change can roll back to a stored checkpoint. One agent no longer relies on files that another agent has already corrupted.

A production system separates confirmed business state from attempt history. A paid invoice or posted journal entry belongs in the first layer. Model calls, retries, and intermediate theories stay in the second. After a failure, the agent rereads confirmed data. An idempotency key protects the bank and accounting system from a second payment.

A checkpoint sits before every irreversible action. Required fields are present, the source has been verified, and the relevant permission is valid. Tests deliberately break a tool and inject plausible bad data. The team tracks correct completion, false completion, and the cost of returning to a clean state.

Story 05

Strict checks account for the gap between 18% and 82% accuracy in AuditFlow

A financial agent can write a convincing explanation for a wrong number. AuditFlow is designed so persuasive prose cannot decide the outcome. The language model guides the search while ordinary programs perform calculations with unambiguous results.

The system works with XBRL reports, where each number connects to an accounting concept, a period, and a unit. One graph stores US reporting rules. A second graph is assembled from the filing. Links between them lead from a reported value to the rule that constrains it.

Two agents inspect the same case from different directions. One starts with the rule and looks for the required facts. The other starts with reported values, compares periods, and recomputes relationships. A senior agent sees the disagreement and requests a specific missing piece of evidence.

Each route must run the required tools: sign checks, calculation trees, dimensional sums, and fact history. An early answer returns to the worker with a list of skipped checks. The final package contains the reported value, expected value, evidence, and full action path.

The model has freedom where search is needed. It chooses the next fact and decides which discrepancy deserves investigation. That freedom ends at computation and at the write boundary. Code recalculates a sum, access control checks the user’s role, and a missing step blocks completion.

The authors disabled system components one at a time. Removing deterministic checks dropped joint accuracy on 67 tasks from 82.09% to 17.91%. Invalid results rose from 1.49% to 35.82%, and accuracy on two quality rules reached zero. Removing the mandatory tool gate lowered joint accuracy to 74.63% and raised invalid results to 17.91%.

The architecture transfers directly into a production finance product. Before posting an entry, the system assembles source fields, the rule version, and outputs from control programs. An open exception stays in the same package. A person sees one connected case, makes the decision, and signs it with a name and timestamp.

This trail can expose a failure before a customer finds it. A reviewer opens the exact number, reruns the calculation, and sees which check allowed the error through. The next test comes from that case and remains useful after the underlying model changes.

Chapter 04

How a small center can run a large portfolio

Story 06

Lagercrantz runs 85 companies with a 22-person headquarters

Lagercrantz owns 85 niche companies. Roughly 22 people work at headquarters. The center handles capital and portfolio visibility. Each company leader owns customers, people, and daily decisions.

The group buys durable businesses with operating margins commonly at 15% to 20% or higher. A deal often costs four to eight times annual EBITDA. Private equity firms may offer around eleven times. Owners still choose Lagercrantz because the company keeps its brand and management, and the asset receives a permanent home.

No integration army arrives after closing. A small divisional team supports the company leader while the center sets principles for allocating capital. Country managers appeared as the geography expanded. The structure protects headquarters attention for decisions that can change the whole portfolio.

Responsibility for an acquisition continues after signing. The person who led the deal usually joins the acquired company’s board and stays close to the result. Significant purchases pass through several levels of discussion. At a pace of ten to twelve deals a year, the group accepts that some decisions will fail.

The model gives an accounting rollup a useful boundary. The center owns shared metric definitions, data quality, and capital allocation. The local team keeps the customer relationship and the authority to resolve a rare exception. A common rule spreads across the portfolio after the group knows where it worked and who will own its use.

AI can help a small center read the portfolio without a stream of slide decks. The system brings close delays, manual corrections, and recurring exceptions into one queue. A leader opens the exact case with the underlying data. A successful route can move to a neighboring company while preserving the author’s decision and the conditions under which it worked.
