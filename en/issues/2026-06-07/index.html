Chapter 01

Who Will Capture the Gains

Story 01

AI: The New Electricity or a Global Vendor Lock-In?

I watched an excellent conversation between Dwarkesh Patel and economists Alex Imas and Phil Trammell. One simple question stayed with me: when AI becomes ubiquitous, who will capture most of the gains?

The guests offer a vivid fork in the road. AI could become the new electricity, available to almost everyone. Or it could become the new social media, where billions use the technology but a handful of platforms collect the money for years.

Social networks have a particular kind of gravity: their size is the product. Advertisers go where the largest audience already is. The more people join, the more valuable the network becomes—and the harder it is for a newcomer to catch up.

It is not yet clear whether AI has the same gravity. Possible sources include vast computing capacity, the best engineers, and feedback from millions of users. The major labs are clearly trying to turn these advantages into a lead that cannot be closed quickly.

The scale of the race is already striking. Epoch AI estimates that US investment in AI data centers, equipment, and networks reached roughly 0.8% of GDP in the first quarter of 2026. Satya Nadella says Microsoft built more Azure capacity over the past 15 months than it did in the platform’s first 15 years.

But the size of the investment does not guarantee permanent power. Open models are developing along their own trajectory. Algorithms are getting cheaper. And infrastructure such as DriveNets makes it possible to mix accelerators from different manufacturers and switch suppliers more easily.

My bet is that AI will ultimately look more like electricity. There will be leaders, and for a time the gap between them and everyone else may be enormous. But I still do not see a force that will bind the entire world to a single lab forever. The most interesting battle for money will probably move higher up the stack—to products, customer relationships, and ways of working that are hard to copy.

Story 02

The Market May Grow. What Happens to People?

The same podcast makes another important point. What happens to an industry and what happens to the people inside it are two different questions. A market can grow even as the amount of work left for people shrinks.

For industries, everything comes down to demand. If a product becomes dramatically cheaper and people are willing to buy much more of it, automation expands the market. Economists call this demand elasticity. The term is complicated; the idea is simple: how much more do we want something when it gets cheaper?

Food has a limit. People will not start eating lunch twenty times a day just because groceries have become cheaper. That is why rising agricultural productivity eventually caused the industry’s share of the economy to fall sharply.

Cloth once went the other way. In the nineteenth century, it became roughly 50 times cheaper, people bought far more clothing and household textiles, and the number of weavers initially rose. Saturation came much later.

The central question for AI is the same. Legal help is currently too expensive for vast numbers of people. We also seem to need far more software than we know how to create. And health is almost impossible to saturate: people want to live longer, look better, and be sick less often.

But a growing market does not guarantee growing employment. AI can take on an ever-larger share of the work while the total volume of services continues to rise. The result is a richer industry with a falling share of human hours.

When the dust settles, people will probably retain a special premium where the human being is part of the product. Psychotherapy, art, fine dining, personal brands. Doctors and other professions built on trust and personal responsibility may belong here too. We do not yet know.

A rather uncomfortable experiment lies ahead: almost every profession will have to prove all over again why the customer needs a human being in particular. We will see how much we truly matter to one another.

Chapter 02

What People and Companies Will Retain

Story 03

Who Will Train the Next Expert?

There is one kind of work everyone wants to hand over to AI first: dull, repetitive, junior work. And that is where the trap lies. This work did more than produce an output. It trained the experts of the future.

Mark Masson makes the point through the example of accounting firms. Over the years, a young professional would see hundreds of documents, errors, and unusual cases. Along the way, they learned to spot anomalies, ask questions, and recognize when a formally correct answer was dangerous.

If AI quietly takes over all the routine work, the savings will appear immediately. The problem will emerge later—when the firm needs senior professionals and discovers there is nowhere for them to come from. You cannot accumulate ten thousand hours of experience if all the easy hours have disappeared.

That means training will have to be designed separately. Not as quarterly lectures, but inside the work itself: first, a person solves a training case; then they compare their answer with the system, work through the exception, and only afterward earn greater autonomy.

The main asset here is neither the course nor the knowledge base. It is a stream of thoroughly examined mistakes through which a newcomer gradually earns the right to take responsibility for a real outcome.

The most paradoxical result of automation may be this: the less junior work remains, the more companies will have to spend on developing junior people.

Story 04

Why Customer Errors Could Become Your Most Valuable Asset

Imagine an agent that confidently completes sixteen steps, then loses an important constraint on the seventeenth and does entirely the wrong thing. For the customer, it is a failure. For a good product, it is rare and extremely valuable training material.

The When Tools Fail study reveals an uncomfortable fact: large models learn to perform a routine task noticeably faster than they learn to recover from an unexpected failure. They struggle especially with errors that look plausible and do not come with a flashing red light.

If a product records the agent’s path, the system state, the expected outcome, and the moment a person stepped in, the error stops being a support anecdote. It becomes a test. After every fix, that test can be run again to prevent the old problem from returning.

This is where a product develops real memory. The general-purpose model is available to many. But thousands of proprietary cases with a clear answer to “what went wrong here?” stay inside the company and gradually teach the system to work exactly as its customers need it to.

Satya Nadella calls these private evaluations a company’s intellectual property. I prefer a simpler test: swap out the underlying model. If the product loses almost no quality, the value has genuinely accumulated around the model rather than living inside it.

There is also a next step, still experimental: a repeatable skill can potentially be compressed into a small adjustment to the model itself. But the central idea already works without magic. A well-understood mistake should make the product stronger forever.

Story 05

The Accounting Firm Stops Selling Hours

Most of an accounting firm’s economics still works in a simple way: people put in hours, and the firm sells those hours to the client. AI breaks that link before it changes the name of the service.

Isaac Heller breaks down a hypothetical $100,000 audit and shows where the work goes. Documents must be collected and organized. Working papers must be linked to evidence. Some procedures must be repeated during review. Errors found late must be corrected all over again.

Each of these operations leaves a trail. Where did the number come from? Which rule was triggered? What did not reconcile? Who reviewed the exception, and why did they make that decision? If this trail lives alongside the work, the firm gains more than an archive of documents: it gains a repeatable way to carry the job through to completion.

AI can then take over preparation, reconciliation, and repeated checks. A person steps in when there is an exception or professional judgment is required. Their work begins to look less like manual production and more like responsibility for edge cases.

Then the price starts to wobble too. If a task that used to take hours now takes minutes, selling it under the old timesheet model becomes increasingly difficult. The service-as-software conversation puts it more bluntly: the client is buying a finished result, not the team’s time.

My bet is that the main asset of a strong accounting firm will be neither its workforce by itself nor a license for the latest model. It will be its accumulated way of working—documents, rules, checks, exceptions, and decisions linked into one clear chain. That is the chain the firm can improve with every new client.

Chapter 03

How Companies and Software Are Changing

Story 06

How Acquisitions Become a Group, Not a Junkyard

Buying a lot of good firms does not automatically create a good group. Without a shared system, the owner ends up with a collection of companies that share a balance sheet but do almost nothing to help one another.

Sweden’s Lagercrantz offers an interesting contrast. Its CEO, Jörgen Wigh, describes a group of more than 85 companies, a 22-person headquarters, and more than 90 acquisitions. The center stays small, while most decisions are made close to the customer.

But autonomy does not mean freedom from rules. The center retains control over target selection, capital allocation, and performance requirements. The local team knows its market, but it cannot explain away weak numbers indefinitely as quirks of the business.

For an accounting group, I would add one more layer: shared rules for exchanging data and a simple way to spread acquired technology. If one firm learns to close the books faster or review documents more efficiently, that capability should reach the others without a year-long integration project.

This is where an important fork appears. You can buy firms for their current profit. Or you can also buy new production capabilities: technology, sales, customer trust, or the ability to serve more companies with the same team.

A good group therefore looks less like a holding company with a giant spreadsheet and more like a learning machine. Every acquisition brings something useful to the rest, while the center preserves what makes customers value the local teams.

Story 07

AI Agents Are Breaking Software Built for People

Almost all modern software is designed for a patient human: they open a screen, perform a few actions, and leave. An AI agent behaves differently. It can launch hundreds of tasks in parallel and return to them again and again.

Satya Nadella describes a world where hundreds of agent sessions run at the same time. In that world, an ordinary chat list looks like an airport control room replaced by a stack of conversations.

Access changes too. People are often given an entire role: accountant, manager, administrator. Agents need much more precise boundaries. This record may be read. This change may be prepared. But a payment may be sent only after approval.

Memory is another problem. The Emergent team saves disk and memory snapshots so an agent can resume its work where it stopped. Without that state, a long task easily turns into Groundhog Day: the system searches again for what it has already found and forgets decisions already made.

The K-BrowseComp and SubtleMemory studies reveal a similar weakness. An agent may find the correct fact but lose the relationship, role, or source on the way to its final answer. A large number of search actions does not mean the work is under control.

Software for agents will therefore look less like a collection of screens and more like a work-management environment. It will show the goal, the state of every task, exceptions, evidence, and the points where a person must make the decision. Selling a single subscription seat also starts to look strange when hundreds of digital workers are laboring in that seat.

Chapter 04

When AI Gets Permission to Act

Story 08

The Danger Begins When AI Gets the Keys

As long as AI merely offers advice, its mistake remains text on a screen. The real danger begins later—when it is given a password, a terminal, and permission to change things.

Anthropic describes a case called GTG-1002. Claude Code was launched in a security-testing environment and connected to tools that could investigate another system and execute commands. At that point, the model stopped being a conversational partner and became a participant in the attack.

Each individual step looked familiar: gather information, find credentials, invoke a tool, alter the system. It was the chain that proved dangerous. The agent could move from one capability to the next on its own.

The conclusion is simple: a model’s risk is determined not only by how intelligently it reasons. What matters more is which doors it has been allowed to open—and whether whatever happens behind them can be undone.

Permissions should therefore be extremely narrow. Reading a document is one permission. Preparing a change is another. Sending money, deleting data, or executing a command is something else entirely. The most dangerous step is best stopped before execution and shown to a person.

A black box is also essential: what the agent saw, what it decided, which tool it invoked, and who approved the action. Without that history, all that remains after a failure is the system’s confident assurance that everything went well.

We are used to debating how intelligent AI is. In the real world, another question matters more: what exactly is it allowed to do while our backs are turned?
