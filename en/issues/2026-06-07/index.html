Chapter 01

Where an agent becomes dangerous

Story 01

An agent becomes dangerous when it gets the right to act

Danger grows when a model gets tools and the right to act inside an environment. In the period studied, the share of actors with at least a medium level of AI enablement rose from about 33.5% to 56.1%, a 1.7x increase in less than a year. The breadth of the technique set is already a weak way to separate high risk: its correlation with the risk score was low, r = 0.27.

The steadier signal sits in the wrapper around the model. Dangerous actors build a chain where AI connects separate stages of an attack and moves through them with minimal human input. In the same picture, AI appears more often deeper inside the attack: use for finding accounts in a compromised environment grew by 8.9%, while AI phishing fell by 8.6%. The focus is shifting to tasks that need time, observation, and decisions along the way: accounts, lateral movement, privilege escalation.

GTG-1002 gives the densest example. The actor used Claude Code on Kali Linux. Open penetration testing tools were connected to it as MCP servers. In this mode, AI ran commands and reasoned about the attack environment on its own. This means tool use and command execution become a separate product security boundary, especially when the model can act autonomously inside an environment.

The numbers show where to test this boundary. In the sample, 64.7% of threat actors used AI for T1027: XOR/base64 encoding, polymorphic variants, and bypasses of signature-based detection. 30.3% used AI to write malicious code that could be injected into legitimate processes, including process hollowing and DLL injection. These are techniques for running code inside a trusted process. Lateral movement stands out even more: 54 actors with this technique had an average risk score of 56.4, almost 10 points above the 46.8 average.

For an AI product founder and an enterprise deployment leader, the decision changes at the threat model level. You have to look at the agent's permissions: what commands it can run, what tools it can see, how the environment limits are set up, and what the system will allow after a breach. Checks should cover account discovery chains, movement and privilege escalation, tool calls, and post-breach abuse scenarios.

Chapter 02

How to prove quality

Story 02

The agent is tested on your own tasks

An agent changes the environment it works in. Tasks keep going, differ from one another, and drift away from the original set over time. One open benchmark ages fast. The authors of Adaptive Auto-Harness propose a live evaluation loop that collects new tasks and adjusts the test right while the task is being solved.

Control now has a good practical test. A team takes its own private evaluation, swaps model A for model B, and tries to raise the result. The ability to improve quality after the swap shows that the company controls the system around the model. A failed test reveals lost control. This is where the product's engineering advantage appears.

A demo leaves this layer out of view. In one study, agent robustness grew 3.66 times more slowly with model size than the general ability to finish tasks. Coding agents also lost form when they met real work. On tasks from real commits, exact answer match for the pretrained model fell from 45.7% to 31.5% compared with a static set. The specialized Code2LoRA-Static reached 63.8%, 9.9 percentage points above full fine-tuning combined with knowledge base search.

Tool choice needs its own test. In ToolBench, the query often retells the API documentation and gives the model a familiar description. ToolSense forces it to tell apart similar tools by meaning. In the study, configurations with a stronger semantic layer transferred better to unfamiliar queries even after part of the memorized knowledge was damaged. The link between early selection accuracy and later tool retrieval was strong: r = 0.79 at p < 0.001 across 14 configurations.

Parallel agents add another task: state. The Emergent team wanted to run several agents on one snapshot, so it created disk and memory snapshots. Answer quality now sits beside precise control over the version of the world each agent is working with.

A private evaluation can become part of the product's intellectual property. In conversations with builders of agent companies, it sits next to proprietary data, operating modes, traces of human-agent interactions, user behavior, and feedback. These traces show how the company creates value. They can be used to train an agent that knows the routes of a specific company.

A team needs its own set of live tasks, traces from real runs, and a transfer test across models. For tool-using agents, similar APIs by meaning and unfamiliar query wording are added. For parallel agents, exact environment state is added. A model's overall score says little about how this whole system will behave tomorrow morning.

Story 03

A financial agent needs executable checks

In AUDITFLOW, the agent works inside a review loop for structured financial reporting, while executable rules check the result. When researchers removed these checks, final joint accuracy collapsed from 82.09% to 17.91%. The share of invalid results rose from 1.49% to 35.82%. On two data quality rules, accuracy fell to zero.

The risk appears earlier. After the mandatory tool gate was disabled, joint accuracy fell to 74.63%, and the share of invalid results rose to 17.91%. This points to a scenario where the agent finishes the task before gathering enough evidence and passing the required check.

The architecture becomes fairly concrete. The model runs the task and chooses the path. The rule lives in an executable tool whose result can be repeated. The experiment results support a practical conclusion: the final answer is safer to treat as ready only after tool-based checking and external validation.

For this kind of financial loop, the launch boundary sits before formal task closure. The agent enters the real process with mandatory executable checks. Disabling them increases invalid results and lowers accuracy on formal rules.

Chapter 03

Where the money and value go

Story 04

AI turns into a construction project

AI compute infrastructure has become the leading source of growth in private investment in the United States. According to Epoch AI, since 2023 its share of the country's GDP has more than doubled. The calculation includes servers, data center sites, and networks inside the United States. The metric is limited to construction inside the country.

The pace is visible in Azure. Over the past 15 months, Microsoft has built more Azure capacity than in the platform's first 15 years. Compute has stopped being a line in a cloud bill and has become a capital race with land, buildings, servers, and network.

Inside this race, the stack is changing. Bessemer writes that the market is ready for a split stack and heterogeneous compute. The industry calls the next shift an inflection toward inference: running a trained model has become the largest and most complex part of the AI compute workload.

This shifts a product team's attention. Training cost appears when the model is created. Inference creates a recurring compute load and needs a separate economic calculation: how much a completed task costs, how the price changes as the chain grows, and which parts of the stack can be replaced.

The capital picture has an important economic correction from Dwarkesh Patel's conversation with Alex Imas and Phil Trammell. The capital stock can grow quickly while the price of capital goods relative to consumer goods falls even faster. New capacity creates new ways to use it. The relative cost of compute capital can fall during the cycle.

If algorithmic improvements get close to a limit, pressure will move into hardware and infrastructure. In one discussion, the thesis was that training compute has already grown a trillionfold and could grow another thousandfold over the next three years. For the economics of an AI product, three different numbers are needed here: training price, inference price, and margin sensitivity to the chosen stack.

Story 05

Cheap intelligence moves value closer to the work

Open models may lag the frontier by six or nine months. If this gap holds, almost everyone will get access to comparable intelligence. The phrase "we have a strong model" will lose weight fast. The money will stay with those who turn intelligence into finished work.

The central economic question in Dwarkesh Patel's conversation concerns the future rent from AGI. Concentration would keep the gains close to the technology. An electricity scenario would spread much of the value across factories, services, and people who learn how to work with it. Ubiquitous social networks produced another outcome: the rent went to the platform.

This gives a useful question for any AI business: what gets stronger here with every new customer? A competitor will soon have access to the base model. The workflow, data, and finished result protect the product more strongly than the model itself.

Cheap automation affects markets in different ways. Work is made of tasks. AI can take nine out of ten, the human can focus on the last one, and the service can get cheaper. If demand grows enough, this can lead to more hiring. For software, this scenario looks plausible: the cheaper it gets, the more new software people want to create. Food has a physical ceiling. Long-term demand reacts more strongly than short-term demand. The physical limit remains: at some point a person is simply full.

There is another scarcity: human involvement. The relational sector here means goods and services where human participation itself is part of the value of the result. A medical service can be almost fully automated, while the doctor remains to deliver the diagnosis and support the patient. The buyer may value that exact moment.

An experiment with art prints shows the mechanism. Participants paid real money and valued a single print made by a human far above the AI version. When they were told there were 500 such prints, the price of the human work fell sharply. The feeling of connection with a specific artist disappeared. The valuation of the AI work stayed the same: from the start, people saw it as a mass-produced good.

An AI product now has two different bets. In the mass segment, value gathers around completed work and accumulated customer context. In the relational segment, the human remains a visible part of the service. The second bet holds only where replacing the human noticeably impoverishes the result.
