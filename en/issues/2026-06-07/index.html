Chapter 01

The Cost of Scale

Story 01

AI Buildout Shows Up in GDP—and Hits the Grid

For a long time, the cost of AI looked like an internal concern for technology companies: the price of a chip, server rental, or a single model query. Now the scale of the buildout is visible from the outside. Epoch AI estimates spending on AI-related data centers, computing equipment, and networks at roughly 0.8% of U.S. GDP in the first quarter of 2026.

Investment in all computing infrastructure reached roughly 1.5% of GDP. The average in 2015–2022 was around 0.7%. By Epoch's calculations, this buildout became the main source of growth in private investment in the country. This is a share of current investment, not proven returns from AI.

The estimate is assembled from three incomplete maps. Data center construction is visible in Census Bureau statistics. Epoch calculates spending on computers and peripherals as the amount above the previous trend. Network equipment has to be estimated from IDC data and company reports. Some of the spending may have gone to ordinary cloud services, while overseas construction by U.S. companies is not included.

On the same day, Alphabet filed a package with the SEC that allows it to raise up to $80 billion through several instruments. The company expects capital expenditures of $180–190 billion in 2026, against $174 billion in operating cash flow over the preceding twelve months. Even so, it needs access to equity, convertible securities, and debt markets.

The entire $80 billion cannot be attributed to AI. About $30 billion in one of the programs is intended primarily for taxes when shares are transferred to employees, while the remaining funds formally go toward general corporate purposes. The signal is more modest than the headline: the computing race has become large enough to affect the capital structure of one of the world's biggest companies.

The next constraint is at the power outlet. Google and Voltus agreed to assemble up to 100 megawatts of annual flexibility in the PJM power system over three years, drawing on batteries, thermostats, and controllable loads in homes and businesses. Crusoe chose another route: Bergen Engines will supply 438 megawatts of gas-fired generation at data center sites, with another 310 megawatts still recorded in a letter of intent.

Two architectures for the same shortage are taking shape. The first frees capacity on the shared grid by shifting thousands of small loads in sync. The second bypasses the interconnection queue with its own round-the-clock generation. A data center operator becomes a participant in the energy market or the de facto owner of a power plant.

Both energy figures describe future capacity, and 100 megawatts of flexibility cannot be compared directly with 438 megawatts of generation. The main test lies ahead: how much capacity will actually come online, at what price, and what return the computing buildout will deliver. The cost of AI now depends on debt rates and grid design almost as visibly as it does on the next model.

Chapter 02

The Model Enters the Workflow

Story 02

A General-Purpose Model Has Reached the Chemist's Bench

A chemist does not see a molecule with the naked eye. One of the main ways to verify its structure is a nuclear magnetic resonance spectrum: a set of peaks, shifts, and distances between them. Specialized software and highly experienced people know how to turn this pattern into the structure of a substance.

Anthropic gave this work to three versions of Claude. For the forward task, the researchers synthesized 20 new compounds from four families that appeared after the training-data cutoff date. The models received a structure and predicted its spectrum; the result was then compared with a measurement and two conventional programs, ChemDraw and MestReNova.

Claude Opus 4.7 was the best at predicting proton chemical shifts, with a mean absolute error of 0.079 ppm, or parts per million. For carbon, its error was 1.37, close to MestReNova's 1.48. In the more difficult part, where the fine structure of a peak matters, the models came within 0.5 hertz in roughly 80% of cases. The conventional programs achieved 26–35%.

The reverse task is closer to a real investigation: the molecule has to be reconstructed from its formula and spectrum. Claude received 15 problems and three attempts at each. It identified all eight simpler compounds in every run. Among the seven difficult ones, where the starting material for the reaction was also shown, four were correct three times and the other three twice.

The leaderboard alone does not explain the mechanism. The working hypothesis is that a language model connects names, structures, reactions, and spectra in a single space and can move between these representations. It does not need a separate interface for every transition. Specialized software calculates according to predefined rules; the model also proposes what structure the numbers describe.

If the result withstands independent verification, part of the expensive manual interpretation will turn into a rapid first pass. A chemist will be able to spend more time on disputed peaks, impurities, and designing the next experiment. That is the value of a professional tool, even if the final answer is still confirmed by a person and a laboratory.

For now, there is little basis for claiming a broad victory. Anthropic itself conducted and described the study, and the sample consists of 20 compounds and 15 reverse problems. There are no two-dimensional spectra, stereochemistry, complex natural substances, or dirty mixtures here. In the difficult problems, the models were given the starting material, which greatly narrows the search.

This experiment is best read as an early crossing of a boundary. A general-purpose model matched specialized tools on a small, clean portion of chemical work. The real test will begin on spectra from an ordinary laboratory, where the solvent interferes, the sample is imperfect, and no one knows the correct structure in advance.

Chapter 03

A New Attack Surface

Story 03

A GitHub Comment Learned to Steal Keys

In an ordinary development pipeline, text from an issue remains text. An agent reads it as a task and receives tools with which to act. Microsoft showed how this difference made it possible to hide an instruction for Claude Code GitHub Action in an issue, pull request, or comment and reach build secrets.

The chain began with a command injected into Markdown. The agent was instructed to read `/proc/self/environ`, where a Linux process can see environment variables. Bash commands ran inside Bubblewrap with a sanitized environment, while the Read tool took a different route and could see `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` along with potentially other secrets.

Reading alone was not enough. The researchers transformed the value so that the model would not recognize it as direct key exfiltration and GitHub Secret Scanner would not stop the output. A suitable workflow channel could then carry the data outside. The result was a complete laboratory chain from someone else's text to a secret.

Anthropic closed this path on May 5 in version 2.1.128. Microsoft published the technical analysis on June 5, after the fix was already in place. The analysis reports no mass theft of keys in real projects.

A new class of risk emerges at the point where a system stops distinguishing content from control. To a person, a comment suggests an idea. To an agent, the same comment can become a command if tools, permissions, and an exit to the outside world are available nearby.

The practical rule from the analysis is very specific. It is dangerous to give a single workflow untrusted input, access to secrets, and the ability to change state or send data outside at the same time. Remove any one link, and the complete chain breaks.

This changes how automation in public repositories should be treated. It is not enough to check only the system prompt and shell commands. Every agent tool, its file paths, log, comments, and network exits together form a shared security boundary.

The patched vulnerability remains useful precisely as an X-ray of the architecture. It shows how an innocuous feedback channel becomes a control input when an agent with permissions appears beside it. The next similar flaw may already live in an email, a customer ticket, or a document that the system is used to treating as data.

Story 04

The Danger in an AI Attack Lives Between Steps

A list of a hacker's techniques usually helps establish the level of threat. Anthropic found that this counter begins to mislead when attacks involve AI. A dangerous actor may use roughly as many known techniques as an average one, but connect them into a self-directed chain.

The company studied 832 accounts blocked for malicious cyber activity from March 2025 through March 2026. The sample included cases with enough telemetry to be labeled using MITRE ATT&CK. It is only a subset of all Anthropic blocks, not a map of global cybercrime.

Most often, AI helped prepare an attack. That was true of 560 accounts, or 67.3% of the sample. Another 54 accounts used the model to move laterally inside an already compromised network. The share of actors with an internal risk score at or above average rose from 33% in the first six months to 56% in the second six months.

The depth of use changed as well. Assistance in finding active accounts inside a network increased by 8.9%, while assistance with phishing fell by 8.6%. That shift is consistent with movement from drafting a message to taking action after the initial intrusion.

The orchestration around the model proved to be the most persistent indicator of high risk. Advanced actors make it move through the stages of an attack in sequence, choose the next step based on the situation, and continue with little human involvement. This capability lives between individual techniques, so the conventional matrix barely sees it.

An espionage episode stopped by Anthropic in November 2025 is illustrative. It covered 30 techniques across 13 tactics, which made it look similar to many medium-risk cases. Anthropic's internal methodology gave it the maximum 100 points because the model worked as an autonomous operator and linked the steps into a campaign.

For defenders, this implies a new unit of observation: the sequence of actions and the design of the orchestration. An individual request may look permissible. The danger emerges when one result automatically becomes the input for reconnaissance, privilege escalation, and data theft.

The figures cannot be generalized to the entire internet. Anthropic sees only its own users, selects cases documented in sufficient detail, and applies its own risk scoring. Some of the increase may have resulted from changes in detection or sample composition. But the mechanism is already visible: the autonomy of an attack is measured by the connections between its steps, while current defensive vocabularies mainly describe the steps themselves.

Chapter 04

Work After Acceleration

Story 05

Code Gets Written Faster. The Verification Queue Grows

Fiona Fung, head of the Claude Code team, described a strange outcome of acceleration. Writing code and tests and reworking old parts of the system are no longer the team's main constraint. The work now runs into verification, review, and security.

When an agent produces several working alternatives in minutes, an engineer still has to understand the consequences, choose one, check the boundaries, and take responsibility for the result. Faster generation increases the flow of changes. The build system and the people doing reviews receive more work, even if each individual piece of code was produced faster.

The Anthropic team is responding with a flatter organization and planning as information arrives. The boundaries among product, design, and engineering are becoming more permeable. The volume of code written is losing meaning as a measure of work; what matters more is the time from task to accepted change, the speed at which a new employee gets up to speed, and the quality of the result.

A harsher corporate signal appeared alongside it. GitLab cut about 14% of its workforce, roughly 350 people, and began exiting 22 countries. On its call with investors, management spoke of removing up to three management layers and promised to reinvest most of the savings in research, people, and its own AI tools.

But it is too early to fuse these events into a single story about AI. GitLab may have been conducting an ordinary cost reduction and attaching a contemporary narrative to it. The company itself warns of the risk of operational disruption and does not expect material revenue from its agentic platform in the current fiscal year.

Together, the two observations show a plausible mechanism. Cheap execution reduces the value of passing a task through multiple roles and layers of approval. The value of judgment rises because every rapid result has to be integrated into the system, verified, and signed off under someone's name.

That is why buying coding agents without rebuilding review can easily create a new bottleneck. A team needs strong automated tests, a clear owner for each change, security checks, and the ability to roll back a result quickly. Management work changes too: less routing of information, more shaping of boundaries and decision quality.

Anthropic remains an unusual company, and its account has no control group. GitLab so far shows a management decision, not its outcome. The hypothesis can be tested with more prosaic figures: pull request cycle time, post-release defects, reviewer workload, and the number of changes that had to be rolled back.

Story 06

Half the Tasks Are Automated. The Job Remains

A frightening automation figure often skips over the most important question: has the entire job disappeared? SHRM surveyed 14,245 U.S. workers and estimated that 20% of paid employment is at least half automated. In 21% of cases, AI already performs at least half of the tasks.

A large gap remains before full replacement. For 60.4% of employment, SHRM found at least one barrier that is not resolved by a model's technical capability. The customer wants a person, the law requires a signature, the organization rests on trust, or someone needs to be accountable for the decision. Only 5.1% of jobs are both highly automated and free of such obstacles.

Employment statistics from the same week look contradictory. In May, the U.S. economy added 172,000 jobs, while unemployment remained at 4.3%. Hotels and restaurants, local government, and healthcare grew. Financial employment fell by 22,000.

At the same time, Challenger counted 97,006 announced job cuts. Employers attributed 38,579 of them to AI, or 40%. The technology sector accounted for 38,242 cuts. These figures describe company announcements, not jobs already lost; AI may serve as a convenient label for ordinary cost cutting.

Economists Alex Imas and Phil Trammell suggest looking at complements and demand. If AI takes nine tasks out of ten, the remaining human task may become more valuable because the entire product is now produced faster. But that will happen only where the lower price induces enough additional demand. Invoking the Jevons paradox guarantees nothing on its own.

The result is a market moving at different speeds. An office function may shrink while in-person services continue hiring. Overall employment grows while simultaneously leaving people in a difficult transition: in May, there were 524,000 more long-term unemployed people than a year earlier. The skills of a financial analyst do not automatically become the skills of a nurse or a data center technician.

For a company, it is more useful to break down a specific job than to multiply the percentage of automated tasks by headcount. Where is customer trust required? Who signs off on the result? Which part of demand will grow after the price falls? How will an employee acquire the remaining judgment if the simple training tasks are already performed by a model?

None of these indicators gives a clean picture. SHRM builds its indicator on self-reports and a 50% threshold. Challenger records the reason given by the employer. BLS data are preliminary, and the economists explicitly acknowledge a lack of information about demand elasticity and the movement of tasks. The figures to watch, therefore, are hiring, layoffs, pay, and transitions between occupations. The count of automated tasks does not provide the answer on its own.
