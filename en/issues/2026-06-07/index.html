Chapter 01

Electricity on the Outside, a Platform Within

Story 01

Mass-Market AI Does Not Eliminate the New Rent

If base intelligence becomes cheap, that does not yet mean the gains will be shared equally.

Economists Alex Imas and Phil Trammell offer a useful fork: electricity or social networks. Both technologies spread everywhere. But electricity gave an enormous share of the gains to users, while the network effect of social platforms left rents with a handful of owners. Ubiquity and the distribution of money are different things.

AI already shows signs of both trajectories. Nathan Lambert suggests that customers will pay a closed lab a premium for a small advantage on the most expensive work. For a multitude of ordinary tasks, they will choose a good-enough open model and build their own tool around it.

Even in the second scenario, concentration does not disappear. Research by Roxana Mihet, Kumar Rishabh, and Orlando Gomes using company data from 2000–2023 shows a subtler connection: improvements in AI especially help firms with large stocks of data, while access to already processed data lets firms that are weaker in AI catch up. What matters is not the volume of raw material by itself, but the ability to turn it into usable knowledge.

A mixed outcome may follow. On the outside, the model spreads like electricity. Within individual companies, a small platform arises: more real work produces rare errors, verified corrections improve the process, and the better process attracts still more work.

This loop does not arise automatically, either. Logs may be rubbish, the test may be wrong, and the customer may take back its data. The practical test of advantage is therefore simple: does the accumulated way of working survive a model change and an unfamiliar environment? If not, the rent stayed with the provider or with a fortunate demo.

The issue's central bet is therefore this: AI can become electricity, while proof becomes a platform. The cleanest place to test that idea this week was chemistry, where a beautiful answer quickly collides with physical reality.

Story 02

The Model Proposes a Molecule. The Laboratory Proves It

Nuclear magnetic resonance, or NMR, can be thought of as a molecule's fingerprint. The instrument shows the environment around its atoms, and a chemist reconstructs the structure.

On June 5, Anthropic reported a strong result from the general-purpose Claude Opus 4.7. In a small test of 20 new compounds, it matched or outperformed the specialized MestReNova at predicting spectra. On the inverse task, the correct structure appeared among the top three candidates on every run for eight simple compounds and on most runs for seven complex ones.

But the model did not receive what a laboratory sees. Claude was given HRMS and manually transcribed lists of one-dimensional NMR peaks; for difficult tasks, it was also given the starting substance's SMILES. There were no raw instrument files, two-dimensional spectra, mixtures, or stereochemistry. The samples were small, and the test was conducted by the provider itself.

On the same day, an independent group published the SECS system. It begins with raw NMR, infrared, and mass-spectrometry data. This is not a contest with Claude, but the missing half of the workflow: the path from an instrument to a structure that can be verified.

Why is this not a minor detail? In blind trials, full NMR-data processing—from transformation and phasing to peak picking and impurity assessment—took an average of about 84 minutes per task. After that preparation, the system automates the reasoning, but not the whole cycle.

The gap between a clean task and the laboratory proved enormous. On one experimental dataset, the system trained only on simulations produced 0% exact first answers. After fine-tuning on a separate set of 2.37k molecules with experimental spectra from that laboratory, the result rose to 55.8%. On another broad sample of 1,486 recently published real compounds, the first answer was correct in only 10.3% of cases.

The thesis and antithesis do not destroy each other here. The general-purpose model makes it cheaper to translate between representations and propose a candidate. Raw signals return value to the instrument, calibration, a clean sample, confidence assessment, and the choice of the next measurement.

This shift had already happened before today's models. When Pfizer accelerated miniaturized synthesis, purification and analysis became the bottlenecks. The company linked them to NMR in a flow of roughly 36,000 compounds a year. Speeding up one step would not have accelerated the cycle without rebuilding the next one.

A strong AI laboratory will therefore be valuable not because of the size of its archive, but because of the speed at which it produces the next labeled truth: choose a measurement, obtain the raw signal, correct the structure, and preserve the entire path. A competitor will have to reproduce not the data, but the evidence factory itself.

The right metric here is confirmed structures per instrument-day at a fixed risk of error. If a general-purpose model learns to read raw data from different laboratories with equal reliability and no local tuning, this bet will weaken. For now, the week showed the opposite: a cheap guess raises the value of the connection to reality.

Chapter 02

How a Correction Becomes an Asset

Story 03

A Test Turns an Error Into Memory—or a Trap

In an ordinary company, an analyzed case plays the role of a physical experiment: source data, the expected result, the error, and a verified correction.

In AUDITFLOW, the distinction is almost cartoonishly clear. Across 67 financial-reporting tasks, GPT-5.5 paired with executable checks achieved 82.09% joint accuracy on the verdict and the figures. Without them, it achieved 17.91%. The narrow dataset does not measure all of auditing, but it cleanly separates plausible text from a result that has survived rules and arithmetic.

A failure becomes memory only if the original state, the acceptance rule, the reason for the correction, and a repeatable check are preserved. Otherwise, the company is collecting not knowledge, but a set of fortunate final answers.

A recent audit of five popular robot benchmark suites revealed an uncomfortable boundary. A small model with no language understanding came within one percentage point of the best comparable published result on three of LIBERO's four components. On LIBERO, only 19.8% of claimed SOTA improvements could be confidently distinguished from statistical noise.

Sample size was not the only reason. Tests often sat too close to the training data. The robot recognized a familiar scene, the position of an object, or a shortcut to the answer, and looked more general than it really was.

A real contradiction arises. A test must be stable, or versions cannot be compared. And it must change, or the system will learn the exam. The resolution is a permanent core, fresh hidden cases, and testing in an unfamiliar environment.

The asset, then, is not a folder of private tests. The asset is the ability to challenge your own exam: vary the conditions, keep new tasks separate, and investigate discrepancies with external evaluation.

That ability comes at a cost. Fresh cases cannot be used for both training and honest evaluation; an unfamiliar environment is slower than an internal exam. A good testing loop must therefore sometimes stop an attractive improvement in which the team has already invested.

Good memory must survive two replacements: the base model and the familiar environment. If the result remains strong after both, the correction truly has become knowledge. Then the next question is no longer technical: who owns that knowledge?

Story 04

A Correction Becomes Capital

The proof loop feeds on rare human corrections. But if the author sees that today's work trace will weaken their position tomorrow, they may stop sharing—and the company will lose its training material.

On June 4, the HBS AI Institute brought an uncomfortable question to the surface: who owns expertise when workplace decisions and exceptions train AI? A separate working paper by Zoë Cullen, Danielle Li, and Shengwu Li describes the mechanism this way: today's trace becomes a system capable of doing the same work tomorrow. The firm gains a new outside option in negotiations with the author of the knowledge.

One part of the mechanism was tested in an experiment with 971 paid Prolific participants. When people were told that their past answers could train a similar AI, 41% refused to provide access to their data for $10, compared with 25% in the control group. This is not the behavior of employees in a real firm. But disclosing the purpose of use genuinely changed willingness to share.

There is a strong antithesis. A study of 5,172 support agents showed that AI can spread experience quickly: productivity rose by an average of 15%, and by roughly 30% for less experienced workers. Newcomers with two months' tenure performed no worse than peers without AI who had more than half a year of experience; some of the skill persisted even during system outages.

But the most experienced agents barely sped up, while the quality of their conversations declined slightly. If strong employees follow an averaged prompt more often, the company gets fewer new solutions. The same loop can train the novice and flatten the peak of practice.

Individual ownership of data does not necessarily resolve the conflict. In the HBS model, workers' contributions are partly substitutable: when one sells their trace, the firm gains strength in negotiations with the others. Collective governance performs better there, but that is a conclusion of the model for now, not an observed market.

A record also preserves an action but often loses the reason, context, and embodied skill. Human training will therefore have to be separated from production: the agent handles the flow, while a specialist first solves selected rare cases independently, then compares the path and explains the exception.

If the firm wants an honest stream of corrections, consent to data collection is not enough. It needs provenance for the contribution, rules for reuse, and a clear share in measurable improvement. Otherwise, the company will build a digital veteran while teaching its best people to remain silent.

Chapter 03

When an Outcome Gets a Price

Story 05

When AI Sells an Outcome, Error Gets a Price

An answer in a chat can be wrong. An invoice processed on the basis of that answer already creates a loss.

On June 4, Deloitte examined how to account for revenue from an AI service that charges for a successful outcome. This is not a statistical study of real contracts, but a set of detailed examples applying accounting rules. Yet they reveal a new object of sale: not a seat in a program and not an attempt, but an outcome that both parties have agreed to regard as complete.

In the invoice-processing example, success occurs only after a correct posting in the ERP, passage through the delegated-authority matrix, and completion of control checks. An agreed window follows: if the transaction is reversed for cause, the outcome does not count. No variable outcome fee accrues for an unsuccessful attempt.

Proof thus turns from an internal check into the boundary of variable revenue: until the criteria are met, the revenue does not arise. But an accounting rule does not determine who compensates for subsequent damage. Liability for a false success is established separately in the contract.

Quanyan Zhu's work on insurance for agentic AI shows why one metric is not enough. The main risk boundary runs between a system that provides information and one capable of changing the external world: sending a message, making a payment, or controlling an object. Authority, an action log, and the cause of the loss matter for assessment, not the model's abstract intelligence.

The market cannot yet price this reliably. Loss data are scarce, liability rules are changing, and a metric can be formally satisfied even though real damage has occurred. Payment for an outcome and payment for an error are different agreements.

The IMF offers a useful separation for payments: intent, authorization, and final settlement are not one step. A probabilistic agent can propose an action, but the right to authorize it and the moment after which the money is irreversibly gone must be rigidly defined.

This model works best for a short outcome that can be verified and reversed. In treatment, strategy, or revenue growth, the outcome takes a long time to appear and the agent's contribution is mixed with other causes. There, outcome-based pricing can easily turn into selecting convenient cases and gaming the metric.

A product therefore needs rules for accepting the outcome and paying for the error: how the outcome is proved, when it is accepted, whether it can be reversed, and who pays for a false success. If the seller is paid for success while the customer bears every accepted error, the new price is merely cosmetic.

This closes the loop of the entire issue. The model supplies cheap capability. The laboratory connects it to reality. The test preserves the correction. The workplace dispute determines who owns it. The contract decides when the outcome is accepted and who bears its cost. The new platform does not arise where AI answers more often, but where someone can prove the answer, learn from an error, and take responsibility for an action.
