Chapter 01

From intelligence to controlled action

Story 01

Value Goes to the Agent Trusted to Act

This week the same conclusion surfaced in three places that rarely speak to one another: enterprise software, robotics, and AI economics. A smart model can already propose a plan. But value appears only when a system gives it the right interface, knows the current state of the world, limits its permissions, and can check the consequences.

In EnterpriseOps-Gym, an agent works inside a live simulation of a company: 164 tables, 512 tools, and tasks spanning HR, support, email, calendar, and IT. A verifier judges not the answer’s elegance but what ended up in the databases. At launch, the best model fully completed about 51% of tasks. In the most policy-heavy areas—HR and IT service management—the result was 26–28%. One wrong status or one extra file zeroed out the whole task.

AutomationBench revealed the same gap across 657 scenarios in forty simulated SaaS applications. The leader completed 48.6% of objectives without breaking a rule. Every model violated business constraints, and finance workflows proved roughly twice as difficult as support and ordinary operations. The question is no longer whether a model can write an email or call an API. It is whether it can preserve a dozen dependent conditions all the way to the correct final state.

In robotics, the mechanism is almost physical. Anthropic let models control a pendulum, quadruped, humanoid, and robot arm. Choosing the force for every motor directly, a model usually failed. Given a ready-made low-level controller, orientation tools, and permission to command at a higher level, the same intelligence could guide a robot along a simple route or move a plate. The authors say the result depends on the body and control interface as much as on the model.

This changes where margins may be captured. Benedict Evans argues that today’s token shortage is temporary and sees no guarantee models will retain strong pricing power. Arvind Narayanan and Akash Kapur describe the labs’ countermove: climb the stack and sell not intelligence but a digital employee embedded in workflows and accumulating tacit company knowledge. Switching its underlying API may be easy. Firing this employee together with its memory will be harder.

The strongest antithesis prevents us from calling the model a commodity already. On AutomationBench, the leading open-weights system scored 27.8% versus the leader’s 48.6%; newer models materially improve high-level robot control. Intelligence quality still creates a large gain. But without an action layer, that gain remains a demonstration rather than an economic result.

The next enterprise-AI debate will therefore be about more than token price and leaderboard rank. Buyers will need context and permission portability, a log of every action, rollback, and an exact boundary at which a decision returns to a person. Raw intelligence will become easier to replace before working memory, permissions, and an accumulated history of exceptions do. That is where the new lock-in appears.

Story 02

A Clinical Trial Becomes a Live System

When people talk about AI in pharma, they usually show a molecule that a model supposedly invented faster than a person. But one of the most expensive bottlenecks comes later: the drug already exists, while data takes weeks to travel from clinical sites to the sponsor, then to analysts and the regulator. The decision is delayed not by the lack of a hypothesis, but by the slow formation of a shared view of what is happening.

Bristol Myers Squibb and Accenture described a different kind of automation this week. BMS deployed more than thirty generative-AI solutions, with Workbench at the center—a system that combines current operational data, the history of past trials, recommendations, and tracked actions by teams. The company links its use to accelerating trials by months.

The most important number is not the number of models. In three months, Workbench usage grew from fewer than one hundred people to nearly nine hundred; more than thirty priority trial teams used the platform, and BMS made it the official system for those trials. This is no longer an assistant that summarizes a document. It is a shared screen on which the organization sees a deviation earlier and assigns an intervention before a delay becomes a quarter.

The regulator is moving in the same direction. Back in April, the FDA announced two pilot trials that send endpoints and signals to the agency in real time. Under the old flow, sites collect the data, the sponsor analyzes it, and only then does the FDA receive the package. The agency wants to shorten that gap and eventually remove the pause between separate phases of development.

The synthesis goes deeper than saying AI accelerates drug discovery. The applied shift is that the trial itself becomes an observable, steerable system. A model gathers fragmented signals, but value comes from a short loop: spot the problem, show its source, assign an owner, check whether the intervention worked. In a field where one Phase III trial can cost tens of millions to more than one hundred million dollars, removing several months matters more than another polished summary.

The limit is easy to see. The BMS account is a joint customer-and-contractor case study with no disclosed counterfactual; the FDA has begun only narrow pilots. We do not yet know whether approvals have accelerated or patient outcomes have improved. The indicators to watch are therefore not the number of AI features, but time from signal to decision, the number of prevented delays, and a verifiable path from recommendation back to primary data.

Chapter 02

Where expensive accountability remains

Story 03

A professional firm will sell accountability, not hours

Hourly billing was more than a convenient tariff. It answered a question the client could not otherwise verify: did the adviser really work hard and conscientiously on my problem? The quality of a complex tax or legal conclusion is opaque, while four hours on an invoice is visible to everyone.

AI breaks this contract from both sides. The client already knows that initial research and a first draft take minutes, so duration no longer proves value. But the firm cannot simply remove the person: in finance and law, an almost-correct answer is still a wrong answer.

An IDC study commissioned by Sage among more than two thousand finance leaders put a price on the contradiction. On average, they spend almost 13 hours a week reconstructing, checking, and defending machine-generated conclusions. Seventy-one percent would reject a system claiming 99% accuracy if it could not explain its decision; 54% would pay more for transparency. The bottleneck is no longer producing an answer, but defending it to an auditor, a board, or a court.

The market already feels the pressure but has not caught up. According to Thomson Reuters, 71% of in-house lawyers expect outside firms to change their commercial models as AI spreads, while only 28% of firms report having changed pricing. Hourly billing still holds most legal spending. This is not the instant death of the old model, but a widening crack between what is counted and where the risk remains.

The boundary becomes clear in a proof-of-cash during deal work. AI can already extract transactions from inconsistent bank-statement PDFs, reconcile them to the general ledger, and surface exceptions. It cannot decide whether revenue will recur after a change of ownership or whether key customer relationships will survive it. The machine removes the data plumbing; the experienced accountant owns the meaning of the deviation.

One tariff therefore separates into two products. Routine research, reconciliation, and document preparation become a fixed-price service or subscription. Judgment, disputed exceptions, and a professional signature are priced separately—as a transfer of risk from the client to the firm. Inside the firm, hours and document volume will no longer be enough: what matters more is the share of conclusions that survive review and lead to a defensible decision.

Story 04

+5.7% hides several different economies

One Bank of Russia number can support both an optimistic and an alarming headline. In June, average daily incoming payments were 5.7% above the first-quarter average. But remove extraction, petroleum products, and public administration, and the result becomes a 1.5% decline.

The difference is not a statistical curiosity. It comes from sharply different sources of demand. Payments in externally oriented sectors rose 25%. Petroleum refining was the main engine, with help from metals and mining. At the same time, investment-demand sectors fell 11.3%, and state-demand sectors fell 11%. Within the investment group, research and development contributed minus 9.5 percentage points in June.

The strongest antithesis prevents us from calling this a general contraction. The consumer group rose 6.1%, and intermediate demand rose 8.9%; electronics and specialized construction supported the investment block. Yet the consumer gain was concentrated too: petroleum refining, financial services, and real estate pulled it up, while retail and wholesale trade made negative contributions.

‘The economy grew’ therefore becomes a poor working assumption for an individual company. An exporter, a bank, and a real-estate-adjacent business may see acceleration. A supplier of research, engineering design, equipment, or work funded from an investment budget is operating in a different market. The average line does not describe either one well enough.

The boundary matters especially here. Payment flows are a fast indicator of cash receipts, not GDP, output, or profit. In July the Bank of Russia updated its outlier treatment and sector weights. These figures do not prove a recession. They prove concentration: a positive headline rests on a narrow set of drivers while large channels of future demand move down.

For planning, the origin of customer revenue matters more than the aggregate figure. If growth continues to live in external demand while the investment channel remains negative, two companies in the same country will describe opposite economic realities—and both will be right.

Chapter 03

When the restriction is built into the model

Story 05

Dangerous knowledge gets an off switch

Ordinary model safety resembles an instruction to a librarian: the book remains on the shelf, but the reader is not allowed to borrow it. A refusal and an external classifier can stop an ordinary request, yet the knowledge remains inside the model. Bypass the protection or fine-tune the model, and it becomes accessible again.

Anthropic and AE Studio showed a different approach this week. During pretraining, GRAM routes updates from virology, cybersecurity, nuclear physics, and specialized code into separate groups of neurons. The general model uses these modules when they are connected. Remove a module, and the corresponding capability leaves with it—not as a rule in the interface, but as a missing part of the weights.

In the experiment, four dual-use categories produced sixteen configurations of one model: each module could be switched on or off. The authors tested sizes from 50M to 5B parameters. Removed capabilities were markedly harder to recover through additional training than after conventional unlearning, while the remaining knowledge was largely preserved. For a set of five profiles, GRAM required about five times less training than separate data filtering for every version.

This is an early result, not a production fuse for Claude. The method has not been tested at frontier-model scale or in a production system; much of the evaluation relies on text-prediction quality rather than real dangerous actions. Related knowledge may prove inseparable, and post-training may mix carefully separated capabilities again. Anthropic states explicitly that GRAM has not been applied to any of its production models.

But the object of governance is already changing. Today a provider says: the model knows, but promises not to answer. In a modular design it could say: this deployment does not contain that knowledge, while a vetted laboratory has received a module with a known hash. If the approach survives scale, AI licenses and audits will describe capability configurations as concretely as access rights and equipment lists.
