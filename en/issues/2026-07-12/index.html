Enterprise automation runs into exceptions and verification

Verification is becoming part of how AI creates value, not just a control mechanism. Yet even the strongest models complete only about half of tasks in privileged mode, while in professional services AI separates routine work from judgement, which remains hard to predict.

Published · 10–12 min read
Chapter 01

Main changes

Story 01

Scaling AI runs into exceptions, people, and verification

This week’s publications converge on the idea that deploying AI in organisations is shaped by redesigning work and verifying results, not by access to a model.

BCG places AI-first companies in the group that uses foundational tools based on large language models widely across the organisation. In the same assessment, BCG says that the other 95% of companies have not even enabled employees to experiment. According to Nevin, at scale, 70% comes down to people and change, 20% to data and technology, and 10% to algorithms. Among nearly 6,000 surveyed technology workers, 22% fear being replaced by AI and 51% fear being required to do more for the same pay.

An analysis at Gap Inc. found that employees who treated AI as a probabilistic partner requiring context, iteration, and feedback performed individual work better than those expecting a reliably consistent answer to a prompt. A recent MIT study called verifying AI outputs not only a compliance function, but a core part of creating value from AI. According to Accounting Today, weakening workers’ ability to verify AI outputs will limit the value they can get from AI. a16z puts it this way: enterprise automation comes down to handling exceptions. Future Firm argues that without standardisation, automation merely accelerates chaos rather than creating an efficient firm. Lilian Weng notes that even if harness improvements are built into foundation models, the need to set goals and context will not disappear.

Story 02

An agent is assessed by the completed task, not its answer

In privileged mode, even the strongest models completed only about half of 1,117 tasks.

EnterpriseOps-Gym-AA tests whether an agent can complete a multi-step task in a live enterprise environment, where actions are irreversible and success depends on policy compliance, not simply producing an answer. In this test, Claude Fable 5 passed 79% of individual checks, while models completed only 51% of tasks in full. Even the strongest models completed only about half of 1,117 tasks in oracle mode.

For Muse Spark 1.1, the rise in AA-Omniscience was attributed to abstaining from answers, not greater accuracy. Epoch AI cautions that, because its assessment uses pull-request diffs and metadata rather than full agent transcripts, prior METR validation does not transfer directly to these results.

Story 03

Finance agents move towards connected operations

The AI semantic layer is described as a central hub with a unified graph of financial knowledge and a mechanism for resolving context.

Accounting Today links autonomous finance to a shift from the tense manual close of a reporting period to continuous real-time financial operations. In Deloitte’s description, the AI semantic layer brings together enterprise master data, financial subledgers, the general ledger, and the enterprise performance management platform. Xero says accountants and small businesses can create AI agents without writing code, connecting Xero to the third-party apps they already use.

Xero says JAX will analyse a customer’s payment history and automatically execute tailored debt-collection plans. Xero also says that JAX will identify anomalies that manual review may miss.

Story 04

Inference economics pushes labs up the application stack

Unlike the cloud model, generative AI incurs consumption costs from the first use.

McKinsey contrasts the cloud model, with high upfront costs and slowly rising per-user spend, with generative AI, which is consumption economics from the first call. Rob May writes that a fixed subscription with variable token costs caps revenue per customer but not costs; the most active 5% of users can break the unit economics of the whole customer base. The authors of AI as Normal Technology consider a business built on charging for frontier-model inference unusually hard to sustain. In their view, AI inference lacks geographic differentiation, so companies can in effect serve the entire market.

AI labs have already begun moving up the application stack to create value; many of these strategies are borrowed from enterprise software. Enterprise software has sustained gross margins of 75% or more for decades. The authors of AI as Normal Technology see the state in which labs sustain high gross margins through compute scarcity as temporary.

Story 05

AI changes the professional-services contract, not just the rate

In complex tax matters, AI speeds up routine research and drafting, but analysis requiring professional judgement remains hard to predict.

An Accounting Today author divides most complex tax matters into routine research and drafting, which AI has made fast and inexpensive, and analysis requiring professional judgement, which remains hard to predict. In the author’s view, clients encounter AI on their own, outside the actions of their advisers; the information asymmetry that supported hourly billing is disappearing. Firms with serious AI capabilities, the author estimates, can now predict the cost of routine work better than they could five years ago, changing their assessment of risk. Advocates of value-based pricing note that it shifts execution risk from the client to the firm, and firms are not set up to take on that risk.

In the author’s view, the hourly model endured because it answered a question of trust that nothing else resolved as clearly. Hourly billing made it difficult to price work requiring professional judgement separately, because research hours and advisory judgement were combined on the same invoice. Belinda Pestana says EY clients want a share of the benefits from the company’s day-to-day use of AI. The author stresses that rewriting the terms of an engagement agreement is harder than adjusting a rate.

Story 07

Faster coding does not mean faster research as a whole

Among surveyed engineers and researchers, METR found an average self-reported speed-up of roughly twofold.

Code volume captures the marginal value of code through changes in the allocation of time. According to METR, among survey participants, a broad range of engineers and researchers, the average self-reported speed-up was around twofold. In a CES economic model with homogeneous code, the estimate U ≈ M^β ≈ 2.8 is robust whether code and non-code work are complements or substitutes.

If AI speeds up research outside of writing code by at least 1.3 times, the Cobb-Douglas estimate rises from 2.83 to 3.22. METR notes that developers’ preferences to use AI made it impossible to conduct a randomised controlled trial to measure self-reported acceleration. Under the authors’ stated assumptions, Anthropic’s overall R&D acceleration threshold would require very high automation of code writing.

Story 08

Modular knowledge removal differs from “unlearning”

In GRAM experiments, four dual-use categories allowed a model to be configured in 16 ways after a single training run.

GRAM adds extra neurons to every layer of a standard transformer and separates them into modules, one for each dual-use category. In the experiments, four dual-use categories allowed GRAM to configure a model in 16 ways after a single training run, by enabling or disabling each category. In this test, removing a module did not reduce the model’s overall performance.

By contrast, the post-training method of “unlearning” merely suppressed knowledge: it was easy to restore with a small amount of further fine-tuning. In a test of restoring removed knowledge by training on a small amount of harmful data, GRAM resisted this about as well as data filtering.
