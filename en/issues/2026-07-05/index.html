Chapter 01

When output stops building capability

Story 01

A novice produces the result before becoming an expert

At a large call center, an employee with two months of experience was given AI suggestions. By the productivity measure, they caught up with a colleague who had worked without them for more than six months. It looks like a sharp acceleration in learning. But the figure measures resolved customer issues, not the person’s ability to handle a new case without the assistant.

A study of 5,172 customer-support agents in The Quarterly Journal of Economics offers a strongly optimistic result: with AI, they resolved 15% more issues per hour on average. The gain reached roughly 30% for less experienced employees and 36% for the bottom fifth by skill. The authors also found evidence of faster learning and better English fluency.

This week, Deloitte showed the other side of the same effect. The firm mapped 19,000 work tasks to skills and found a dangerous gap: AI more often takes the work that builds beginner and intermediate proficiency, while human expertise is still needed at the top level. An organization gets a stronger novice today while shortening the path by which that person should become a senior professional tomorrow.

Laboratory and classroom experiments show that the gap is real but not inevitable. In a study of nearly a thousand high-school students, a standard GPT-4 interface sharply raised performance during practice, but after the assistant was removed the group scored 17% below the control on independent work. In a small randomized experiment with a new Python library, AI users learned less about the concepts, code reading, and debugging; full delegation was associated with the weakest learning.

The strong antithesis matters more than the frightening number. The same high-school experiment largely removed the harm when the tutor gave teacher-written hints instead of a ready answer. In another randomized study, a carefully designed AI physics tutor gave students more than twice the median learning gain in less time than an active-learning class.

The causal variable, then, lies in the design of the assistance. An answer engine optimizes the completed task and lets the user move the thinking outside themselves. A tutor leaves the person with meaningful effort, surfaces the mistake, and gradually reduces the hint. The same model can accelerate both the habit of delegating understanding and the development of skill.

A company will need a second scale next to productivity: can the employee explain the decision, find a hidden assumption, recover without AI, and transfer the principle to an unfamiliar case? If only completed tasks are counted, the savings appear immediately and the expert shortage is discovered during a rare failure, when there is no longer time to learn.

Story 02

Repetition alone does not turn into experience

Something similar happens to the model. You can give it the same task thirty times, allow it to keep notes, and explicitly ask it to learn from mistakes. None of this ensures that the thirtieth attempt will be smarter than the first.

Epoch AI tested this in Earthborne Rangers, a complex and obscure card game. Agents received the rules, a card database, a map of the world, and either 10 or 30 playthroughs. They were told that the last attempts mattered and were encouraged to preserve lessons. Newer models started stronger than older ones, but scores on the final attempts barely rose relative to the first.

The failure was not limited to tactics. The agent chose a deck before every game but did little exploration across the space of 32 coarse archetypes. Even a detailed guide from an experienced player added only a few completed objectives out of 21. More actions reproduced the original strategy instead of searching for another one.

This is a narrow negative test, not proof that models are generally incapable of learning. The working harness may have retrieved memory poorly, the game may have required an awkward form of exploration, and code, multiple agents, or human reviews could have changed the result. That boundary leads to the more interesting conclusion.

In Andrej Karpathy’s autoresearch, repetition is organized differently. An agent changes one file containing model-training code, runs a fixed five-minute experiment, compares one metric, and preserves the change only if it improves the result. Roughly a hundred comparable attempts fit into a night. Here, the system is not merely asked to remember the experience: the experience physically changes its next version.

At the AI Engineer World’s Fair, this outer layer was already being described as a product of its own. The inner loop performs work for a user. The outer loop studies failures, turns them into tests and checking rules, changes the agent recipe, and preserves the human decisions from which a new version emerged. Without that layer, the work log remains an archive rather than learning.

People and machines need the same transition: an attempt, a distinguishable outcome, a review of the cause, and a change to the next attempt. The carriers differ. A person needs a task before the hint, feedback, and permission to fail in a simulation. An agent needs persistent memory, variation in search, a measurable result, and a selection mechanism.

The new scarcity is a company’s ability to turn exceptions into the next level of work. The differentiating forecast is simple: strong teams will measure not the volume of stored logs but the share of repeated failures, the number of new evals per expert intervention, and improvement by the next version on unfamiliar cases.

Chapter 02

Where verifiable judgment becomes more valuable

Story 03

A verifiable answer gets cheaper faster than a good question

In Dwarkesh Patel’s conversation with Grant Sanderson, mathematics looks like an early map of the future of intellectual work. Models have already crossed a boundary that was convenient to regard as distant until recently: they can do more than restate what is known and can produce a new, verifiable result.

In May, an internal OpenAI model found a counterexample to Erdős’s unit-distance conjecture, which specialists had regarded as plausible for nearly 80 years. External mathematicians checked the proof and wrote an analysis. The model connected a problem in geometry with ideas from algebraic number theory, even though it was not a specialized system for this problem.

This week, Sanderson proposed a useful boundary for the shift. A proof converts well into a task: the claim is given, the chain can be checked, and computation can be directed toward a clear outcome. A good conjecture or definition is harder. The reward does not tell the machine which new concept will open a field or which of thousands of truths are worth pursuing at all.

It does not follow that questions will belong to people forever and answers to machines. The model already found a connection that many strong mathematicians had not seen. It will probably move higher up the chain as well. But the near-term scarcity is visible now: choosing a fruitful direction, recognizing the value of an unexpected result, and assembling a new picture of a subject from individual proofs.

There is also a strong antithesis to the fear of incomprehensible machine mathematics. This counterexample could be rewritten in ordinary mathematical language; it did more than close a problem and exposed a connection between fields. A machine result can increase human understanding if someone performs the work of checking, explaining, and selecting.

A research team will therefore look less like a queue of people manually proving assigned lemmas. Its throughput will increasingly be set by the quality of its portfolio of questions and by the speed with which a machine result becomes shared understanding. The scarcity is moving toward the editorial work at the frontier of knowledge.

Story 04

An image of a receipt no longer proves a purchase

A receipt long functioned as a small piece of reality: crumpled, carrying the store address and the correct amount. That image can now be produced in seconds, and a coffee stain only makes the fabrication more convincing.

On AppZen’s platform, no AI images appeared among receipts already flagged as fraudulent in March 2025. By mid-May 2026, their share had reached 70.8%. Behind that figure were 1,471 receipts from 745 employees at 174 companies, claiming roughly $148,000 in total.

The denominator is essential here. This is not 70.8% of all corporate expenses or even the share of all fraudulent transactions. It is the composition of receipts that AppZen’s system had already classified as suspicious. The fraud-detection vendor is an interested source, while a separate Emburse survey in which four in ten American workers said they had generated a false receipt is based on self-reporting.

But the early mechanism is visible without a sensational estimate of scale. Older template-based fakes in AppZen’s data averaged $182, while AI receipts averaged about $100. When creating each document is nearly free, fraud becomes smaller and higher-volume and more easily passes below the threshold for manual review.

Financial control therefore moves from the plausibility of the image to the consistency of the event. A system cross-checks the payment, merchant, time, geography, reuse of the document, and the employee’s history. The receipt itself changes from evidence into one assertion among several independent traces.

If the shift persists, the winners will not be the most attentive people inspecting pixels. Receipts linked to a payment token or a merchant record will appear, and automatic reimbursement without an independent trace will become rare. Generative AI devalues the document and raises the value of provenance.

Chapter 03

A different order for reducing risk

Story 05

A reactor proves the physics before seeking a commercial license

Late on June 30, the Unity microreactor at Idaho National Laboratory reached criticality: its chain reaction became self-sustaining. It was the third unique American design to cross that threshold within one month under authorization from the U.S. Department of Energy.

The speed looks almost implausible. According to the industry publication POWER, Unity went from the start of its project with the laboratory to the criticality experiment in roughly 150 days. Antares Mark-0 and Valar Ward250 had reached criticality earlier in June; the latter was built outside a national laboratory.

The main caveat changes the meaning of the news. Unity conducted a zero-power criticality experiment. The test confirmed the neutron-physics calculations but produced no electricity, useful heat, or sustained power. It is not yet a power plant or proof of commercial economics.

The shift lies in the order in which risk is reduced. The new practical lane uses DOE authority for research and demonstration to build a prototype and obtain data before the full commercial process. The U.S. nuclear regulator, the NRC, is observing the work and is already holding pre-application discussions with Deployable Energy about siting, construction, and operation of a future commercial version.

An accelerated demonstration does not transfer into a license automatically. Full-power and safety testing, commercial NRC authorization, fuel, manufacturing, and the economics of a repeatable product remain ahead. Fast criticality makes the answer to ‘does the physical design work?’ cheaper while leaving open ‘can it be repeated safely and profitably?’

The differentiating forecast is not a near-term flow of electricity. If the new order works, the next NRC applications will arrive with data from already-built DOE prototypes, and investors will begin to assess two speeds separately: months to physical proof and years to a licensed, repeatable product.
