AI is moving into operating systems, and it requires a new operational discipline

This week, the sources converge on one point: agents are gaining access to actions and data, but durable results depend on an operating model, explicit knowledge, feedback, and verification. At the same time, barriers to automation are falling, demands for observability are rising, and the need for professional judgment is not going away.

Published · 10–12 min read
Chapter 01

Main changes

Story 01

Scaling AI has become an operating-model challenge

The sources describe a shift from deploying individual tools to redesigning processes, platforms, and governance structures.

Deloitte’s authors assess the next stage of transformation this way: scaling AI is no longer a technology challenge, but an enterprise operating-model challenge. In their survey, 75% of leaders believe operating models need to change over the next 12–18 months to create more value, yet only about a quarter are doing so continuously or dynamically.

Deloitte also argues that the most effective approach is not standalone tools alongside legacy systems, but AI capabilities embedded in the platforms, processes, and controls already in use. This is why assigning a role to AI cannot be automatic: according to the same source, the decision depends on context, and redesign should be led by people who know which processes to preserve and where AI creates value.

Story 02

Agents that take action need a control layer

Agents are increasingly able to act inside systems and write data; observability and verification are becoming part of how they operate.

According to Yaron, agents have moved from reading, summarizing, and drafting to taking action inside systems. Citing Amplify data, Latent Space reports that among teams using agents, 89% said their agents can write data; a year earlier, 52% of teams could say the same.

A long-running cloud agent has no ordinary cycle in which warnings and errors reach a human. Cursor, The Pragmatic Engineer reports, regularly asks the model for information about issues and passes it to the infrastructure team. As agents become more autonomous and more integrated with tools, the authors of the VERA study propose moving from static benchmarks to modular, executable testing infrastructure.

Story 03

Expertise remains the bottleneck in automation

Automating entry-level tasks can weaken the practical paths to expertise, even though human context remains necessary for high-quality work with AI.

Deloitte warns that as AI takes on more entry-level tasks, organizations risk losing the pathways that turn newcomers into experts. Those pathways matter because effective use of AI tools often requires deep domain expertise, and at higher levels of skill, human expertise remains necessary.

Much experience is tacit: it has been passed on through apprenticeship, mentoring, and learning on the job. Without access to such knowledge, new workers face serious obstacles. In Richard MacManus’s view, an early system should eventually draw tacit knowledge and workflows out of people, rather than trying to automate everything at once.

Story 04

Plausible AI advice does not remove professional responsibility

In professional advisory work, the central risk is not only an error, but a convincing unverified result that a client or firm may rely on.

An Accounting Today author calls the most troubling feature of AI-generated tax recommendations their convincing appearance of professionalism. The same author believes the main threat to the profession is not AI replacing certified accountants, but clients’ inability to distinguish technically accurate advice from advice that merely sounds plausible.

According to Accounting Today, relying on an unverified AI output gives a firm no liability protection, regardless of whether the firm created that output itself. A SOC 2 report assesses a provider’s internal security measures and operating practices, but it does not establish whether the firm had the legal right to give it client information.

Chapter 02

Additional signals

Story 05

Routing and cost are becoming part of agent design

Greater autonomy does not mean compute costs no longer matter: simple and complex tasks increasingly require different models and different cost-control mechanisms.

Models often perform lengthy reasoning even for simple requests, creating substantial compute costs without improving accuracy, Google DeepMind researchers note. A router can therefore send simple support-request classification to a small or local model and reserve expensive tokens for requests that genuinely need them.

Artificial Analysis reports that at the maximum effort level, Anthropic Sonnet 5 used roughly six times more agentic steps in GDPval-AA than at low effort. But a routing layer does not eliminate the architectural bet: if a product is deeply built around one model provider, moving away from it requires redesigning the product.

Story 06

Software factories promise continuity, but not maturity yet

Asynchronous agents are gaining infrastructure for long-running work and progress toward finished changes, but the software-factory model itself remains an unfinished market practice.

The Pragmatic Engineer describes cloud agents working in isolated virtual machines with a full development environment. Thanks to asynchronous work with their own tools, they can spend longer progressing toward merge-ready changes without intervention. But long-running agents create distinct engineering challenges: for example, execution must be moved between nodes when one ends while work is underway.

According to a Latent Space source, a factory works most effectively when it receives context from operating systems and is integrated into all of an organization’s workflows. Jeffrey Huntley also warns that software factories have not yet become a solved market problem; it is more useful to treat them as a direction for engineering work than as a ready-made replacement for a team.
