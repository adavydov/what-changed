Chapter 01

Who owns the working environment

Story 01

Content became model infrastructure

Thomson Reuters launched its own language model, Thomson, starting from a strong open foundation and investing $40 000 000 in training, compute, and talent. The company emphasizes not general scale but full ownership and control, with training shaped by its professional content, tools, and editorial expertise.

Less than 10% of the Thomson Reuters corpus has been used so far. In internal early evaluations, the model is comparable to leading general systems on a range of tasks and improves on its base in complex instruction following and dense domain text. Its first deployment is tabular analysis in CoCounsel Legal.

The next day, Google Cloud showed another form of the same verticalization: Gemini Enterprise for Legal. It is not a separate legal model but a packaged environment of skills, industry agents, document and matter-system connectors, primary legal sources, and a common audit control plane.

The two launches complicate the familiar choice between building a model and calling an external API. The real unit of advantage is broader: corpus rights, editorial labels, domain evaluation, connectors, access policies, and position in the workflow. The model inside that stack can still be one of several.

For a company, the practical question is not “can we train an LLM?” but “which part of our environment cannot be reproduced by one API?” If the answer is only the brand, a private model is unlikely to pay. A closed corpus, experts, and repeatable evaluation can make specialization change economics and control.

Both companies are describing their own products. Thomson Reuters does not publish a standardized independent comparison here, while Google Legal is in preview and provides no measured customer outcomes. The materials reveal an architectural choice and vendor bet; they do not yet prove superiority in real legal work.

Story 02

The model assembled its own planet

Google Research introduced the Planetary Prediction Engine, an experimental system that takes a natural-language request through the geospatial cycle from data discovery and cleanup to features, training, evaluation, and a report. The primary bottleneck had been not the model but weeks of manual assembly across fragmented datasets.

The system divides work into 3 modules: selecting signals and geographic constraints, building a consistent dataset, then comparing model families and checking overfitting. Stages exchange opaque handles to artifacts instead of serializing arrays into prompts, so the context window does not become the data store.

In an Ebola-outbreak tracking task, the system reported 83.3% recall, finding 15 of 18 newly affected zones across 5 consecutive weeks. That was 10.3 percentage points above the public reference used for comparison. In other tests, it combined structured indicators with geospatial embeddings.

The object being automated has changed. The agent does not merely apply a prepared table to a prepared model; it constructs the measurement environment itself. Value moves into the source catalog, spatial join rules, protection against temporal leakage, and a verifiable route from request to report.

For an organization, this makes the data platform part of the AI product. The requirement is not endless exports into a chat but machine-readable datasets, stable keys, temporal boundaries, and artifacts that can be checked again. Without them, autonomous analysis remains a polished interface over manual preparation.

This is an early Google project and a preprint by the system's authors. The comparison covers selected tasks and depends on Google Earth AI, Data Commons, and Earth Engine infrastructure. The reduction from weeks to minutes is a developer claim; it does not measure deployment, data errors, or the cost of a wrong humanitarian decision.

Chapter 02

How the world becomes readable

Story 03

Glucose required two time scales

Google Research introduced GlucoFM, a compact foundation model for continuous glucose monitoring. Instead of one sequence, it runs two streams: a slow path captures persistent metabolic trends, while a fast path captures short deviations. The architecture acknowledges that one sensor carries signals moving at different speeds.

Pretraining used 109,066 hours of unlabeled data and 477 participant or session records. The model was then tested across 14 cohort–clinical-task combinations. Average PR-AUC rose from 54.7 for the strongest matched baseline to 58.8: a gain of 4.1 points, or about 7.5% relative to that baseline.

The authors separately predicted post-meal response across 874 paired meals from 34 participants. Mean absolute error was 21.88 mg/dL versus 22.90 for the best baseline. The difference is modest, but it shows one representation serving both risk classification and personalized short-horizon dynamics.

The lesson extends beyond medicine: continuous data does not become useful automatically when a model receives more context. Someone must choose the time scale, separate background from event, and decide which segments should aggregate. The signal representation becomes as much an asset as the sensor readings.

A product built on telemetry, payments, or behavior should inspect its own pipeline. If one model sees a raw stream at a single scale, it may miss slow drift or dissolve rare events. Architecture should reflect the physics and rhythm of the process, not the convenience of one table.

This study is not a clinical authorization or diagnostic test. The pretraining population contains only 477 records, devices and cohorts differ, and sensors produce missingness and artifacts. The Google authors measured gains on selected tasks; transfer to another population requires separate validation.

Story 04

Hardware gained machine-readable boundaries

Anthropic previewed the Model Hardware Standard. It reduces incompatible instrument interfaces to a common driver where an agent reads state and acts through basic read and write operations, while device characteristics, safety limits, and emergency stops are declared next to the hardware.

In a Carnegie Mellon laboratory, an agent used that layer to connect a liquid handler, plate reader, robotic arm, and cameras across several computers. The authors report that a serial-dilution experiment ran about 3 times faster than the earlier process. The major saving came from integration, not pipette speed.

QuEra applied the same approach to a quantum computer's laser system. A conventional deterministic controller written with agent assistance returned the system to target in 695 of 700 trials. Most faults cleared in under 6 seconds and hard ones in 10–14 seconds, compared with 5–10 minutes for a specialist.

The sequence matters: the model explored the rig and helped write the controller, but did not remain reasoning inside every production cycle. Experts set scope, reviewed the steps, and defined success; the output was inspectable code. Accumulated tacit knowledge became a repeatable execution environment.

For a factory or laboratory, documentation moves to a new place. A runbook should describe not only a human procedure but state, permitted commands, units, interlocks, and recovery. AI can then assemble verifiable automation without receiving unbounded authority over the physical world.

MHS remains a research preview and is not yet an available open standard. Anthropic and partners report the figures, not an independent evaluator. Not every instrument has a programmatic interface, and spatial or physical failures still need experts. A machine-readable boundary reduces integration debt; it does not replace engineering safety.

Chapter 03

Who owns accumulated context

Story 05

Memory became a separate product policy

Anthropic unified Claude's cloud memory across chat and Cowork. The important change is not remembering itself but moving accumulated context into a separate Topics list: a user can inspect, edit, or delete every item. Context stops being a hidden side effect of conversation history.

Sensitive topics such as health and beliefs remain excluded until the user separately enables them. Memory is on by default for Free, Pro, and Max, but off by default for Team and Enterprise. The same mechanism now starts with different policies in personal and organizational environments.

This shows that durable context is a data plane of its own. It needs inventory, editing, deletion, scope, and a conservative default where one person handles corporate or other people's information. Chat history by itself is not such a policy.

A team should separate evidence, working preferences, and sensitive facts. Evidence needs lineage; preferences need an owner and lifetime; sensitive facts need a separate basis for retention. When everything enters one memory, convenience becomes an implicit access decision.

The release note describes the interface and defaults but does not measure memory accuracy, false topics, or actual leakage risk. Settings can change, and a visible list does not prove completeness of every internal representation. It is a useful governability pattern, not evidence of error-free memory.
